\section{Ajtai's Worst-Case to Average-Case Reduction}
\label{app:ajtai}

This section provides a detailed proof sketch of
\cref{thm:ajtai} (Sect.~\ref{subsec:ajtai}), which
establishes that solving the Short Integer Solutions problem on
a random matrix is at least as hard as solving worst-case
lattice problems.

\begin{theorem}[Ajtai~\cite{Ajtai1996}, Micciancio--Regev~\cite{MicciancioRegev2007}]
\label{thm:ajtai-app}
Let $n, m, q$ be integers with $m = \bigO(n \log q)$ and
$q = \poly(n)$, and let $\beta < q$.  If there exists a
probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm~$\mathcal{A}$ that,
given a uniformly random matrix
$\mathbf{A} \getsr \Zq^{n \times m}$, finds a non-zero
$\mathbf{x} \in \Z^m$ with $\norm{\mathbf{x}} \leq \beta$ and
$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \pmod{q}$ with non-negligible
probability, then there exists a probabilistic polynomial-time
algorithm that solves $\gamma$-$\sivp$ on every
$n$-dimensional lattice, where
$\gamma = \beta \cdot \widetilde{\bigO}(\sqrt{n})$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The reduction receives a worst-case lattice $\lat \subset \R^n$
(given by some basis $\mathbf{B}$) and must find $n$ linearly
independent vectors of norm at most
$\gamma \cdot \lambda_n(\lat)$.  It proceeds in three stages.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Stage 1: Gaussian sampling on the
target lattice.}
Using the iterative Klein/GPV sampler, the reduction draws
lattice vectors from the discrete Gaussian distribution
$D_{\lat, s}$ with parameter $s$ chosen above the
\emph{smoothing parameter}~$\eta_\varepsilon(\lat)$
(\cref{def:successive-minima}).
Recall that $\eta_\varepsilon(\lat)$ is the smallest~$s$ such
that
$\rho_{1/s}(\lat^* \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}) \leq \varepsilon$,
where $\rho_t(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(-\pi \norm{\mathbf{x}}^2 / t^2)$.
Above the smoothing parameter, the reduction modulo the
fundamental parallelepiped of $\lat$ produces a distribution
that is statistically close to uniform over the parallelepiped.
This property is crucial: it allows the reduction to
\emph{embed} the target lattice into a random-looking
$\textsc{SIS}$ instance.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Stage 2: Constructing a random SIS
instance.}
The reduction builds the matrix $\mathbf{A} \in \Zq^{n \times m}$
column by column.  For each column~$j$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Sample $\mathbf{v}_j \leftarrow D_{\lat, s}$.
\item Compute
  $\mathbf{a}_j = \mathbf{B}^{-1} \mathbf{v}_j \bmod q$.
\end{enumerate}
Because $s \geq \eta_\varepsilon(\lat)$, the vectors
$\mathbf{a}_j$ are within statistical distance~$\varepsilon$ of
uniform over~$\Zq^n$.  Hence $\mathbf{A}$ is
indistinguishable from a genuinely random matrix, and the
$\textsc{SIS}$ oracle~$\mathcal{A}$ applies.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Stage 3: From a SIS solution to short
lattice vectors.}
The oracle returns a non-zero $\mathbf{x} \in \Z^m$ with
$\norm{\mathbf{x}} \leq \beta$ and
$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \pmod{q}$.
Unravelling the construction, this means that the vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w} \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{m} x_j \, \mathbf{v}_j
\end{equation}
lies in $q \cdot \lat$ (it maps to zero modulo~$q$).
Since each $\mathbf{v}_j$ is a Gaussian sample of expected norm
$s\sqrt{n}$ and $\norm{\mathbf{x}} \leq \beta$, a standard
tail bound gives
$\norm{\mathbf{w}} \leq \beta \cdot s \cdot \widetilde{\bigO}(\sqrt{n})$.
The vector $\mathbf{w}/q$ is therefore a lattice vector of
$\lat$ with norm at most
$\beta \cdot s \cdot \widetilde{\bigO}(\sqrt{n}) / q$.

Repeating the procedure $n$ times with independent randomness
(and verifying linear independence, which holds with
overwhelming probability for $m \gg n$) yields $n$
independent short vectors in~$\lat$.  Setting the Gaussian
parameter~$s$ appropriately relative to $\lambda_n(\lat)$
ensures the resulting vectors have norm at most
$\gamma \cdot \lambda_n(\lat)$ with
$\gamma = \beta \cdot \widetilde{\bigO}(\sqrt{n})$, thereby
solving $\gamma$-$\sivp$.
\end{proof}

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{Why this matters.}
Ajtai's reduction is the reason lattice-based cryptography
stands on firmer theoretical ground than RSA or elliptic-curve
cryptography.  An efficient SIS solver would imply an
efficient algorithm for \emph{every} instance of approximate
$\sivp$---a problem that has resisted attack for decades.
The reduction is \emph{classical} (no quantum computation
required), unlike Regev's LWE reduction below.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{Regev's LWE Hardness Reduction}
\label{app:regev-reduction}

This section expands on \cref{thm:regev}
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:regev-reduction}), presenting the main ideas
of Regev's quantum reduction from worst-case lattice problems
to the average-case Learning With Errors problem.

\begin{theorem}[Regev~\cite{Regev2009}]
\label{thm:regev-app}
Let $n \geq 1$, let $q \leq \poly(n)$ be prime, and let
$\alpha \in (0,1)$ with $\alpha q \geq 2\sqrt{n}$.  If there
exists a probabilistic polynomial-time algorithm that solves
decision-$\lwe(n, q, D_{\Z, \alpha q})$ with non-negligible
advantage, then there exists a \emph{quantum}
polynomial-time algorithm that solves
$\widetilde{\bigO}(n/\alpha)$-$\gapsvp$ on every
$n$-dimensional lattice.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The reduction receives a worst-case lattice $\lat$ (given by a
basis~$\mathbf{B}$) and a threshold~$d$, and must decide
whether $\lambda_1(\lat) \leq d$ or
$\lambda_1(\lat) > \gamma d$.  It uses an $\lwe$ oracle and
quantum computation as follows.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 1: Quantum state preparation.}
For each dual lattice vector
$\mathbf{w} \in \lat^*$, consider the Gaussian function
$\rho_r(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{w}) = \exp(-\pi\norm{\mathbf{x} -
\mathbf{w}}^2 / r^2)$ centred at~$\mathbf{w}$.  For a suitable
parameter~$r$, the reduction prepares (on a quantum computer) the
superposition
\begin{equation}
\ket{\psi} \;=\; \sum_{\mathbf{w} \in \lat^*}
  \rho_r(\mathbf{w})\;\ket{\mathbf{w}}.
\end{equation}
This state encodes the dual lattice weighted by a Gaussian
envelope.  Preparing $\ket{\psi}$ is the step that requires
quantum computation; it uses Gaussian superposition sampling
over the lattice, building on the iterative quantum sampler of
Regev.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 2: Quantum Fourier transform.}
Apply the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) to $\ket{\psi}$.
By the Poisson summation formula, the QFT of a Gaussian sum
over $\lat^*$ is a Gaussian sum over the primal lattice $\lat$
with reciprocal width:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{QFT}\ket{\psi} \;\propto\;
  \sum_{\mathbf{v} \in \lat}
  \rho_{1/r}(\mathbf{v})\;\ket{\mathbf{v}}.
\end{equation}
Measuring this state yields a lattice point
$\mathbf{v} \in \lat$ distributed according to
$D_{\lat, 1/r}$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 3: From lattice samples to LWE
samples.}
The classical part of the reduction converts each discrete
Gaussian sample $\mathbf{v} \leftarrow D_{\lat, 1/r}$ into an
$\lwe$ sample as follows.  Choose
$\mathbf{a} \getsr \Zq^n$ and compute
\begin{equation}
b \;=\; \ip{\mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{v}} + e \pmod{q},
\end{equation}
where $e$ is a rounding term arising from the discretisation
of $\mathbf{v}$ to coordinates modulo~$q$.  The distribution
of~$e$ is (close to) the discrete Gaussian $D_{\Z, \alpha q}$
when the lattice sampling parameter~$r$ is set appropriately
in terms of~$\alpha$.  The pair $(\mathbf{a}, b)$ is therefore
an $\lwe$ sample with secret determined by the lattice point.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Step 4: Using the LWE oracle.}
Feed the constructed $\lwe$ samples to the oracle.  If the
oracle distinguishes $\lwe$ from uniform, the reduction can
extract information about the Gaussian width~$1/r$ on $\lat$,
which is directly related to $\lambda_1(\lat)$.  Specifically:
\begin{itemize}
\item If $\lambda_1(\lat)$ is small (the YES case of $\gapsvp$),
  the Gaussian mass concentrates on short vectors, and the
  resulting $\lwe$ samples have a detectable structure.
\item If $\lambda_1(\lat)$ is large (the NO case), the Gaussian
  over $\lat$ is essentially supported on the origin, the
  samples carry no information, and the oracle sees uniform
  pairs.
\end{itemize}
The oracle's ability to distinguish the two cases therefore
solves $\gapsvp$.
\end{proof}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Why the reduction is quantum.}
The quantum step is Step~1: preparing a Gaussian superposition
over $\lat^*$.  No efficient classical procedure is known for
sampling from this distribution on an arbitrary lattice.  The
QFT in Step~2 is the natural quantum analogue of Poisson
summation and exploits the same position--momentum duality
familiar from quantum mechanics (see the grey box in
Sect.~\ref{subsec:regev-reduction}).

Crucially, the \emph{attacker} need not be quantum.  Even a
purely classical $\lwe$ solver would, via this reduction,
yield a \emph{quantum} algorithm for $\gapsvp$.  The quantum
nature is a property of the proof, not of the threat model.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{Classical alternatives.}
Peikert~(2009) and Brakerski et~al.~(2013) later established
purely classical reductions from $\gapsvp$ to $\lwe$, at the
cost of requiring either a super-polynomial modulus~$q$ or a
larger approximation factor~$\gamma$.  For the tightest
parameters used in practice (polynomial~$q$, polynomial~$\gamma$),
Regev's quantum reduction remains the strongest known result.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{Correctness of the Fujisaki--Okamoto Transform}
\label{app:fo-correctness}

This section proves \cref{thm:fo-security}
(Sect.~\ref{sec:fo-transform}): the Fujisaki--Okamoto
transform upgrades IND-CPA security to IND-CCA2 security in
the random oracle model, provided the underlying scheme has
negligible decryption failure probability.

\begin{theorem}[FO Security~\cite{FujisakiOkamoto1999, HofheinzHovemannKiltz2017}]
\label{thm:fo-app}
Let $\Pi = (\KeyGen, \Enc, \Dec)$ be an IND-CPA-secure
public-key encryption scheme with $\delta$-correctness
(i.e., $\Pr[\Dec(\mathsf{sk}, \Enc(\mathsf{pk}, m; r)) \neq m]
\leq \delta$ over the randomness of key generation and $r$).
Let $\Pi' = (\KeyGen', \Encaps, \Decaps)$ be the
FO-transformed KEM (\cref{def:fo-kem}).  Then in the random
oracle model, for any IND-CCA2 adversary~$\mathcal{A}$
making at most $q_D$ decapsulation queries and $q_H$ hash
queries,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{IND\text{-}CCA2}}_{\Pi'}(\mathcal{A})
\;\leq\;
2\,\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{IND\text{-}CPA}}_{\Pi}(\mathcal{B})
\;+\; q_H \cdot \delta
\;+\; \frac{q_D}{|\mathcal{M}|},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ is an IND-CPA adversary with comparable
running time and $|\mathcal{M}|$ is the message space size.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (game-hopping argument)]
We proceed through a sequence of games, starting from the real
IND-CCA2 experiment.  Let $\mathcal{A}$ be an adversary that
receives a challenge ciphertext $c^*$ encapsulating either the
real key $K_0^*$ or a random key $K_1^*$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Game 0 (Real game).}
This is the standard IND-CCA2 experiment for the KEM~$\Pi'$.
The challenger runs $\KeyGen'$, computes
$(K_0^*, c^*) \leftarrow \Encaps(\mathsf{pk})$, samples
$K_1^* \getsr \{0,1\}^\ell$, flips a bit~$b$, and gives
$\mathcal{A}$ the pair $(K_b^*, c^*)$.  The adversary may
query $\Decaps$ on any ciphertext $c \neq c^*$ and the random
oracles $H, G$.  Let $W_0$ denote the event that $\mathcal{A}$
guesses $b$ correctly.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Game 1 (Decapsulation uses table
lookup).}
Modify the decapsulation oracle as follows: instead of
decrypting~$c$ with $\Dec$, scan the random oracle table for
any query $m$ such that $G(m, \mathsf{pk})$ produces
randomness yielding $\Enc(\mathsf{pk}, m; G(m, \mathsf{pk}))
= c$.  If found, return $K = H(m, \mathsf{pk})$; otherwise
return $H(s, c)$ (implicit rejection).

Games~0 and~1 differ only when $\mathcal{A}$ submits a
ciphertext~$c$ that is valid (i.e., $\Dec$ recovers some $m'$
with $\Enc(\mathsf{pk}, m'; G(m',\mathsf{pk})) = c$) but
$\mathcal{A}$ never queried $G$ on $m'$.  In the random oracle
model, $G(m', \mathsf{pk})$ is uniformly random and
independent of $\mathcal{A}$'s view, so the probability that
$\Enc(\mathsf{pk}, m'; G(m', \mathsf{pk})) = c$ holds by
accident is at most $1/|\mathcal{M}|$ per query.  Hence:
\begin{equation}
|\Pr[W_1] - \Pr[W_0]| \;\leq\; \frac{q_D}{|\mathcal{M}|}.
\end{equation}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Game 2 (Abort on decryption failure
of $c^*$).}
Modify the game so that if the message $m^*$ encrypted in the
challenge ciphertext $c^*$ would cause a decryption failure
(i.e., $\Dec(\mathsf{sk}', c^*) \neq m^*$), the game aborts.
This event has probability at most~$\delta$ by the correctness
assumption, so:
\begin{equation}
|\Pr[W_2] - \Pr[W_1]| \;\leq\; \delta.
\end{equation}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Game 3 (Replace $K_0^*$ with
random).}
Replace the real key
$K_0^* = H(m^*, \mathsf{pk})$ with a uniformly random value.
Since $H$ is a random oracle and $m^*$ is chosen uniformly at
random by $\Encaps$, the value $H(m^*, \mathsf{pk})$ is
uniformly random unless $\mathcal{A}$ queries $H$ on $m^*$.
If $\mathcal{A}$ ever queries $H$ on~$m^*$, it has
effectively recovered the plaintext, which breaks IND-CPA
security.  Formally, construct an IND-CPA adversary
$\mathcal{B}$ that simulates Game~2 for $\mathcal{A}$ and
outputs $1$ if $\mathcal{A}$ queries $H(m^*, \mathsf{pk})$.
Then:
\begin{equation}
|\Pr[W_3] - \Pr[W_2]| \;\leq\;
  \mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{IND\text{-}CPA}}_{\Pi}(\mathcal{B})
  \;+\; (q_H - 1)\cdot\delta,
\end{equation}
where the additional $\delta$ terms account for the possibility
that $\mathcal{A}$ queries $H$ on a message $m'$ whose
encryption coincidentally equals $c^*$ due to a decryption
failure event.

In Game~3, both $K_0^*$ and $K_1^*$ are uniformly random and
independent of $\mathcal{A}$'s view, so
$\Pr[W_3] = 1/2$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Conclusion.}
Combining the bounds:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{IND\text{-}CCA2}}_{\Pi'}(\mathcal{A})
&= |2\Pr[W_0] - 1| \\
&\leq 2\,\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{IND\text{-}CPA}}_{\Pi}(\mathcal{B})
  + q_H\cdot\delta + \frac{q_D}{|\mathcal{M}|}.
\end{align}
For ML-KEM, $\delta < 2^{-140}$ and $|\mathcal{M}| = 2^{256}$,
making the loss terms negligible.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{The role of re-encryption.}
The re-encryption check in $\Decaps$ ($c' \stackrel{?}{=} c$) is
what makes Game~1 possible: it ensures that any ciphertext
accepted by $\Decaps$ was honestly generated from a message
known to the random oracle.  Without re-encryption, an
adversary could submit malformed ciphertexts that decrypt to
related messages, gradually extracting the secret key.
Implicit rejection (returning $H(s, c)$ for invalid ciphertexts)
ensures that the adversary cannot even detect whether a
ciphertext was rejected, closing timing side channels.

\section{The Rejection Sampling Lemma}
\label{app:rejection-sampling}

This section proves \cref{thm:rejection-sampling}
(Sect.~\ref{subsec:rejection-lemma}), the key technical tool
that ensures lattice signatures based on the Fiat--Shamir-with-aborts
paradigm do not leak the secret key.

\begin{lemma}[Rejection Sampling~\cite{Lyubashevsky2012}]
\label{lem:rejection-sampling-app}
Let $V$ be a finite set of vectors with
$\norm{\mathbf{v}}_\infty \leq \beta$ for all
$\mathbf{v} \in V$, and let $h : V \to [0,1]$ be a
probability function.  Let $\sigma = \omega(\beta\sqrt{\log m})$
where $m$ is the ambient dimension.  Define
$M = \exp(12\beta/\sigma + 1/(2\sigma^2))$.  Consider the
following experiment:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Sample $\mathbf{v} \leftarrow h$ and
  $\mathbf{y} \leftarrow D_\sigma^m$.
\item Set $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{v}$.
\item Output $\mathbf{z}$ with probability
  $\min\!\bigl(\frac{D_\sigma^m(\mathbf{z})}{M \cdot
  D_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}^m(\mathbf{z})},\; 1\bigr)$;
  otherwise restart.
\end{enumerate}
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textup{(a)}] The probability of outputting $\mathbf{z}$
  (not restarting) in each iteration is at least $1/M$.
\item[\textup{(b)}] The statistical distance between the
  distribution of $\mathbf{z}$ (conditioned on output) and
  $D_\sigma^m$ is at most $2^{-\omega(\log m)} / M$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular, the output distribution is (essentially)
$D_\sigma^m$ regardless of which $\mathbf{v}$ was used.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The argument is a direct calculation exploiting the rapid
decay of Gaussians.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Masking property.}
For any fixed $\mathbf{v}$ with
$\norm{\mathbf{v}}_\infty \leq \beta$, the ratio of the
centred Gaussian to the shifted Gaussian at any point
$\mathbf{z}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{D_\sigma^m(\mathbf{z})}{D_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}^m(\mathbf{z})}
\;=\; \exp\!\left(
  \frac{-2\ip{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{v}} +
  \norm{\mathbf{v}}^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).
\end{equation}
Since $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{v}$ with
$\mathbf{y} \leftarrow D_\sigma^m$, the inner product
$\ip{\mathbf{z}}{\mathbf{v}}$ concentrates around
$\norm{\mathbf{v}}^2$ (which is at most $m\beta^2$).
The condition $\sigma = \omega(\beta\sqrt{\log m})$
ensures that the ratio concentrates in $[1/M, 1]$ with
overwhelming probability.  Specifically, a Gaussian tail
bound gives
\begin{equation}
\Pr\!\left[
  \frac{D_\sigma^m(\mathbf{z})}{D_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}^m(\mathbf{z})}
  < \frac{1}{M}\right]
\;\leq\; 2^{-\omega(\log m)}.
\end{equation}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Acceptance probability.}
The acceptance probability equals
$\EE\!\left[\min\!\bigl(
\frac{D_\sigma^m(\mathbf{z})}{M \cdot D_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}^m(\mathbf{z})}, 1
\bigr)\right] = \frac{1}{M}$
(up to the negligible tail), because the expected value of
$\min(R/M, 1)$ for a ratio $R$ distributed as above is
$1/M$ by construction of~$M$.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Output distribution.}
Conditioned on acceptance, the density of $\mathbf{z}$ is
proportional to
$D_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}^m(\mathbf{z}) \cdot
\frac{D_\sigma^m(\mathbf{z})}{M \cdot D_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}^m(\mathbf{z})}
= \frac{1}{M} D_\sigma^m(\mathbf{z})$,
which is exactly $D_\sigma^m$ up to normalisation.  The
negligible tail where the ratio falls below $1/M$ contributes
at most $2^{-\omega(\log m)}/M$ statistical distance.
Since this holds for every $\mathbf{v} \in V$, the output
distribution is independent of the secret (up to negligible
error).
\end{proof}

\section{Security of Merkle's Signature Scheme}
\label{app:merkle-security}

This section proves \cref{thm:merkle-security}
(Sect.~\ref{sec:merkle}): the Merkle tree construction yields
an existentially unforgeable many-time signature scheme from
any secure one-time signature.

\begin{theorem}[Merkle Tree Security]
\label{thm:merkle-app}
Let $\mathrm{OTS}$ be an EU-CMA$_1$-secure one-time signature
scheme and $H$ a second-preimage-resistant hash function.
Then the Merkle signature scheme with tree height~$h$
(supporting $N = 2^h$ signatures) is EU-CMA-secure.
Concretely, for any forger~$\mathcal{F}$ making at most
$N$ signing queries:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{EU\text{-}CMA}}_{\mathrm{Merkle}}(\mathcal{F})
\;\leq\;
N \cdot \mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{EU\text{-}CMA}_1}_{\mathrm{OTS}}(\mathcal{B})
\;+\; N \cdot \mathrm{Adv}^{\mathrm{SPR}}_{H}(\mathcal{C}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ are efficient
reductions.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof (hybrid argument over leaves)]
Suppose forger~$\mathcal{F}$ outputs a valid forgery
$(m^*, \sigma^*)$ on a message not previously signed.  The
forgery contains an authentication path from some leaf~$i^*$
to the root.  We distinguish two cases.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Case 1: OTS forgery.}
The authentication path uses a leaf OTS key pair
$(\mathsf{sk}_{i^*}, \mathsf{pk}_{i^*})$ that was used in one
of the $\leq N$ signing queries.  In this case, the forger
has produced a second valid signature under a one-time key,
which constitutes an OTS forgery.

To reduce to OTS security, construct $\mathcal{B}$ as follows.
$\mathcal{B}$ receives an OTS challenge public key
$\mathsf{pk}^*$ and must produce a forgery.  It guesses a
random index $i' \getsr \{0, \ldots, N-1\}$, generates all
other OTS key pairs honestly, and embeds $\mathsf{pk}^*$ at
leaf~$i'$.  The Merkle tree is built normally.
When $\mathcal{F}$ requests a signature using leaf~$i'$,
$\mathcal{B}$ forwards the message to its OTS signing oracle.
If $\mathcal{F}$'s forgery uses leaf~$i^* = i'$ (which happens
with probability $\geq 1/N$), $\mathcal{B}$ obtains a valid
OTS forgery.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Case 2: Hash collision on the path.}
The authentication path in the forgery reaches the correct
root $\mathsf{PK}$ but diverges from the honest tree at some
internal node.  That is, there exists an internal node where
the forger's claimed child values
$(L^*, R^*)$ differ from the honest values $(L, R)$, yet
$H(L^* \| R^*) = H(L \| R)$.  This is a second-preimage for
$H$.

Construct $\mathcal{C}$ that embeds its second-preimage
challenge at a random internal node and simulates the scheme
for~$\mathcal{F}$.  The guess succeeds with probability at
least $1/N$ (there are $\leq N$ internal nodes on any
root-to-leaf path times the number of leaves).

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Combining.}
Since every forgery falls into one of the two cases, a union
bound gives the claimed advantage.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{Tightness.}
The factor of $N = 2^h$ in the security loss is inherent in
the guessing step and represents a real cost: a Merkle tree
supporting $2^{20}$ signatures loses 20~bits of security
relative to the underlying OTS.  This motivates the use of
multi-tree constructions (XMSS$^{MT}$, HSS) that split the
height across layers to reduce the effective loss per layer,
as discussed in Sect.~\ref{sec:stateful}.
