\section{Symmetric Cryptography}
\label{sec:symmetric}

Modern cryptography\index{cryptography!symmetric} rests on three equally critical pillars: \emph{confidentiality} (only authorised parties read the data), \emph{integrity} (any tampering is detectable), and \emph{authentication} (the identity of the sender is verifiable).  Symmetric cryptography addresses the first two using a single shared secret key.

\subsection{Block Ciphers and AES}
\label{subsec:block-ciphers}

Confidentiality requires a reversible transformation: a way to scramble data so that only the keyholder can unscramble it.  The simplest useful abstraction is a function that encrypts one fixed-size chunk at a time---a \emph{block cipher}.

\begin{definition}[Block Cipher]
\label{def:block-cipher}
A \emph{block cipher}\index{block cipher}\index{AES} is a pair of deterministic algorithms $(\Enc, \Dec)$ operating on fixed-length blocks, with a key $k \in \{0,1\}^\kappa$:
\[
\Enc_k : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^n, \qquad \Dec_k : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^n, \qquad \Dec_k(\Enc_k(m)) = m.
\]
For every fixed key~$k$, the map $\Enc_k$ is a permutation on $\{0,1\}^n$.
\end{definition}

The \emph{Advanced Encryption Standard} (AES)\index{AES} operates on 128-bit blocks with key sizes $\kappa \in \{128, 192, 256\}$.  Its internal structure consists of $N_r$ rounds (10, 12, or~14 depending on key size), each applying four transformations: \texttt{SubBytes} (non-linear S-box substitution), \texttt{ShiftRows} (byte permutation), \texttt{MixColumns} (linear diffusion over $\mathrm{GF}(2^8)$), and \texttt{AddRoundKey} (XOR with round key).  After 20 years of intensive cryptanalysis, no attack against full AES is faster than brute-force key search.

\subsection{Authenticated Encryption: AES-GCM}
\label{subsec:aes-gcm}

A block cipher alone provides confidentiality for a single block, but real messages span many blocks and face threats beyond eavesdropping---an active adversary can flip bits, reorder blocks, or splice fragments from different messages.  A \emph{mode of operation}\index{mode of operation} extends encryption to arbitrary-length messages, and the most important modes go further: they bind confidentiality and integrity into a single primitive called \emph{authenticated encryption with associated data}\index{AEAD} (AEAD).  The dominant AEAD construction in practice is \emph{Galois/Counter Mode} (GCM)\index{GCM}.

\begin{definition}[AEAD Scheme]
\label{def:aead}
An \emph{authenticated encryption} scheme provides $\Enc_k(N, A, M) \to (C, T)$ and $\Dec_k(N, A, C, T) \to M \text{ or } \bot$, where $N$ is a nonce, $A$ is associated data authenticated but not encrypted, $M$ is the plaintext, $C$ is the ciphertext, and $T$ is an authentication tag.  Decryption returns~$\bot$ if any bit of $(A, C)$ has been modified.
\end{definition}

AES-GCM encrypts in counter mode (CTR) and computes the tag $T$ using a universal hash function GHASH over~$\mathrm{GF}(2^{128})$.  It dominates internet traffic: the cipher suite \texttt{TLS\_AES\_256\_GCM\_SHA384} is the most widely deployed in TLS~1.3.

\subsection{Hash Functions: SHA-2 and SHA-3}
\label{subsec:hash-functions}

Authenticated encryption protects data in transit, but many cryptographic operations need something different: a way to produce a short, unique fingerprint of an arbitrary-length message.  Key derivation, digital signatures, and integrity checks all depend on \emph{cryptographic hash functions}---deterministic, efficiently computable maps that behave, for all practical purposes, like random oracles.

\begin{definition}[Cryptographic Hash Function]
\label{def:hash-function}
A \emph{cryptographic hash function}\index{hash function} $H:\{0,1\}^* \to \{0,1\}^n$ satisfies three properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Preimage resistance:}\index{preimage resistance} given $y$, it is infeasible to find $x$ with $H(x) = y$.
\item \textbf{Second preimage resistance:} given $x$, it is infeasible to find $x' \neq x$ with $H(x') = H(x)$.
\item \textbf{Collision resistance:}\index{collision resistance} it is infeasible to find any pair $(x, x')$ with $x \neq x'$ and $H(x) = H(x')$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The \textbf{SHA-2}\index{SHA-2} family (SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512) uses a Merkle--Damg\r{a}rd construction with Davies--Meyer compression.  \textbf{SHA-3}\index{SHA-3} (Keccak) uses a sponge construction\index{sponge construction} over a 1600-bit permutation, offering a completely independent design lineage.  Both families are considered secure; SHA-3 provides a hedge against a future structural break in Merkle--Damg\r{a}rd.

\subsection{Message Authentication Codes}
\label{subsec:macs}

Hash functions detect accidental corruption, but they cannot detect malicious tampering: an adversary who modifies a message can simply recompute the hash.  What we need is a keyed variant---a function whose output can only be produced or verified by someone who holds a shared secret.  This is a \emph{message authentication code} (MAC).

\begin{definition}[MAC]
\label{def:mac}
A \emph{message authentication code}\index{MAC} is a triple $(\Gen, \mathsf{Tag}, \Verify)$ where $\mathsf{Tag}_k(m)$ produces a tag and $\Verify_k(m, t)$ outputs accept or reject.  Security requires that no efficient adversary, even one who can obtain tags on chosen messages, can produce a valid tag on a new message (\emph{existential unforgeability}\index{existential unforgeability}).
\end{definition}

HMAC\index{HMAC}, defined as $\text{HMAC}_k(m) = H\bigl((k \oplus \text{opad}) \| H((k \oplus \text{ipad}) \| m)\bigr)$, is the standard keyed-hash MAC.  It appears prominently in TLS~1.3 key derivation (HKDF)\index{HKDF}.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{Quantum impact on symmetric primitives.}  Grover's algorithm\index{Grover's algorithm} gives a quadratic speedup for brute-force key search, reducing the effective security of AES-128 from $2^{128}$ to $2^{64}$ operations.  The standard mitigation is to double the key length: AES-256 retains 128-bit security against quantum adversaries.  Hash functions similarly require doubled output lengths.  Symmetric cryptography survives the quantum transition largely intact.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{Public-Key Cryptography}
\label{sec:public-key}

\subsection{The Key Distribution Problem}
\label{subsec:key-distribution}

Before 1976, all cryptography was symmetric, leading to a fundamental paradox: to communicate securely, two parties first had to exchange a secret key over a channel that was already secure\index{key distribution problem}.  At scale, this is impractical.

\begin{example}[The Key Distribution Nightmare]
\label{ex:key-distribution}
If $n$ parties each wish to communicate securely with every other, they require $\binom{n}{2}$ distinct shared keys.  For $n = 1{,}000$ this means $499{,}500$ keys, each needing secure physical distribution.  Adding a single new party requires $1{,}000$ additional key exchanges.
\end{example}

Historical solutions---diplomatic pouches, pre-distributed code books, key distribution centres---were expensive, unscalable, and fragile.  The emerging internet of the 1970s demanded something fundamentally different.

\subsection{One-Way Trapdoor Functions}
\label{subsec:trapdoor}

The key distribution problem seems inescapable if encryption and decryption require the same key.  The breakthrough insight of the 1970s was that the two operations need not be symmetric: if computing a function is easy but inverting it is hard---unless one holds a secret ``trapdoor''---then the function itself can serve as a public key.

\begin{definition}[One-Way Trapdoor Function]
\label{def:trapdoor}
\index{one-way trapdoor function}
A function $f: X \to Y$ is a \emph{one-way trapdoor function} if:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Easy to compute:} given $x$, computing $f(x)$ is efficient.
\item \textbf{Hard to invert:} given $y = f(x)$, finding $x$ is computationally infeasible.
\item \textbf{Trapdoor:} there exists secret information $t$ that makes inversion efficient.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

In 1976, Diffie and Hellman\index{Diffie--Hellman} proposed separating the key into a public component (for encryption or verification) and a private component (for decryption or signing)~\cite{DiffieHellman1976}.  This idea---\emph{public-key cryptography}\index{public-key cryptography}---resolved the key distribution problem and made the internet possible.

\subsection{RSA}
\label{subsec:rsa}

Diffie and Hellman described the \emph{concept} of public-key cryptography in 1976, but did not provide a concrete system.  The first practical realisation came two years later from Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman~\cite{RSA1978}\index{RSA}, who found a trapdoor function hiding in elementary number theory.

The design intuition is this: multiplying two large primes is trivial, but recovering them from the product is---as far as anyone knows---extraordinarily hard.  RSA exploits this asymmetry.  The product $n = pq$ is made public; anyone can use it to encrypt.  But computing the decryption exponent requires knowing $\phi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$, which is easy if one knows $p$ and $q$ and apparently intractable otherwise.  The trapdoor is the factorisation itself.

\begin{definition}[RSA Key Generation]
\label{def:rsa-keygen}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choose two large primes $p, q$ (each $\approx 1024$ bits for RSA-2048).
\item Compute $n = pq$ and $\phi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$.
\item Choose $e$ with $\gcd(e, \phi(n)) = 1$; the standard choice is $e = 65537$.
\item Compute $d \equiv e^{-1} \pmod{\phi(n)}$.
\item Public key: $(n, e)$.  Private key: $d$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Encryption and decryption are modular exponentiations:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rsa}
\Enc(m) = m^e \bmod n, \qquad \Dec(c) = c^d \bmod n.
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[RSA Correctness]
\label{thm:rsa-correct}
For any $m \in \Z_n^*$, decryption recovers the plaintext:
\begin{equation}
(m^e)^d = m^{ed} = m^{1 + k\phi(n)} = m \cdot (m^{\phi(n)})^k \equiv m \pmod{n},
\end{equation}
where the last step follows from Euler's theorem\index{Euler's theorem}: $m^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}$ whenever $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.
\end{theorem}

Textbook RSA as described above is \emph{insecure} in practice: it is deterministic (identical plaintexts yield identical ciphertexts) and malleable (given $c = m^e$, an adversary can compute $(2m)^e = 2^e \cdot c$).  Real deployments use \emph{Optimal Asymmetric Encryption Padding} (OAEP)\index{OAEP}, which randomises the input and achieves chosen-ciphertext security in the random oracle model.

\subsection{Diffie--Hellman Key Exchange}
\label{subsec:dh}

RSA solves encryption and signatures, but it does not directly address the original key distribution problem: how can two parties who have never met agree on a shared secret, communicating only over a public channel?  The Diffie--Hellman (DH) protocol\index{Diffie--Hellman!key exchange}, published in the same landmark 1976 paper that introduced public-key cryptography~\cite{DiffieHellman1976}, provides an elegant answer using modular exponentiation.

\begin{definition}[Diffie--Hellman Protocol]
\label{def:dh}
Let $p$ be a large prime and $g$ a generator of the multiplicative group $\Z_p^*$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Alice chooses a secret $a \getsr \{2, \ldots, p-2\}$ and sends $A = g^a \bmod p$.
\item Bob chooses a secret $b \getsr \{2, \ldots, p-2\}$ and sends $B = g^b \bmod p$.
\item Both compute the shared secret $K = g^{ab} \bmod p$: Alice computes $B^a$ and Bob computes $A^b$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

An eavesdropper observes $(g, p, g^a \bmod p, g^b \bmod p)$ but must compute $g^{ab} \bmod p$---the \emph{Computational Diffie--Hellman} (CDH) problem\index{CDH problem}, which is believed to be as hard as the discrete logarithm.  Note that DH provides no authentication: without additional measures, it is vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks.

\subsection{Elliptic Curve Cryptography}
\label{subsec:ecc}

Both RSA and Diffie--Hellman achieve security through the difficulty of number-theoretic problems in $\Z_n^*$ or $\Z_p^*$, but they pay a steep price in key size: 3072-bit keys for 128-bit security.  In the mid-1980s, Koblitz~\cite{Koblitz1987} and Miller~\cite{Miller1985}\index{elliptic curve cryptography} independently discovered that the discrete logarithm problem is dramatically harder in the group of points on an elliptic curve, allowing the same security from keys roughly ten times shorter.  Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) replaces the multiplicative group $\Z_p^*$ with this richer algebraic structure.

\begin{definition}[Elliptic Curve over a Finite Field]
\label{def:elliptic-curve}
\index{elliptic curve}
An \emph{elliptic curve} $E$ over $\F_p$ (with $p > 3$) is the set of points $(x, y) \in \F_p \times \F_p$ satisfying the Weierstrass equation
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weierstrass}
y^2 = x^3 + ax + b, \qquad 4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0,
\end{equation}
together with a distinguished \emph{point at infinity}~$\mathcal{O}$.  The set $E(\F_p)$ forms an abelian group under a chord-and-tangent addition law, with $\mathcal{O}$ as the identity element.
\end{definition}

The group law is defined geometrically: to add two distinct points $P$ and $Q$, draw the line through them; it intersects $E$ at a third point $R'$, and we define $P + Q$ as the reflection of $R'$ across the $x$-axis.  Point doubling ($P + P$) uses the tangent line at~$P$.  Translated into field arithmetic over $\F_p$, these operations are efficient.

\emph{Scalar multiplication} $kP = P + P + \cdots + P$ ($k$ times) is computed in $\bigO(\log k)$ group operations via double-and-add, but recovering $k$ from the pair $(P, kP)$ is the \emph{elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem} (ECDLP)\index{ECDLP}.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Equivalent security levels for RSA/DLP and ECC key sizes (in bits)}
\label{tab:key-sizes}
\begin{tabular}{p{3cm}p{3cm}p{3cm}p{3cm}}
\hline\noalign{\smallskip}
\textbf{Security (bits)} & \textbf{RSA modulus} & \textbf{DLP ($\Z_p^*$)} & \textbf{ECC key} \\
\noalign{\smallskip}\svhline\noalign{\smallskip}
80  & 1\,024 & 1\,024 & 160 \\
128 & 3\,072 & 3\,072 & 256 \\
256 & 15\,360 & 15\,360 & 512 \\
\noalign{\smallskip}\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The dramatic key-size advantage (Table~\ref{tab:key-sizes}) has made ECC the dominant choice for modern protocols: TLS~1.3 mandates ECDHE key exchange (typically over Curve25519 or P-256) and supports ECDSA signatures.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The hybrid approach.}  In practice, public-key cryptography is never used to encrypt bulk data directly---it is too slow.  Instead, modern protocols employ a \emph{hybrid} design: an asymmetric algorithm (ECDHE, RSA) establishes a shared symmetric key, and a fast symmetric cipher (AES-GCM) encrypts the actual data.  This division of labour---asymmetric for trust establishment, symmetric for throughput---pervades every protocol discussed in this chapter.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{Digital Signatures}
\label{sec:signatures}

Public-key encryption and key exchange solve confidentiality, but a critical piece of the security triad remains: how does one prove, to anyone, that a given message was produced by a specific party and has not been altered?  Symmetric MACs (Section~\ref{subsec:macs}) provide integrity between two parties who share a key, but they cannot convince a third party---both parties can produce the same tag.  Digital signatures\index{digital signature} solve this by binding the authentication to an asymmetric key pair: only the private-key holder can sign, but anyone with the public key can verify.  This property---\emph{non-repudiation}---makes digital signatures the foundation of software distribution, financial transactions, and, as we shall see in Section~\ref{sec:pki}, the certificate chains that underpin internet trust.

\subsection{Syntax and Security}
\label{subsec:sig-syntax}

Before examining concrete schemes, we fix the interface that every signature scheme must implement and the security property it must satisfy.

\begin{definition}[Digital Signature Scheme]
\label{def:signature}
A \emph{signature scheme} is a triple $(\KeyGen, \Sign, \Verify)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\KeyGen() \to (pk, sk)$: generates a key pair.
\item $\Sign(sk, m) \to \sigma$: produces a signature on message~$m$.
\item $\Verify(pk, m, \sigma) \to \{0, 1\}$: outputs~$1$ if and only if $\sigma$ is a valid signature on~$m$ under~$pk$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[EUF-CMA Security]
\label{def:euf-cma}
\index{EUF-CMA}
A signature scheme is \emph{existentially unforgeable under chosen-message attack} (EUF-CMA) if no efficient adversary, even after obtaining signatures on polynomially many messages of its choice, can produce a valid signature on a new message.
\end{definition}

EUF-CMA is the standard security notion for signatures in practice; all schemes discussed below target this level of security.

\subsection{RSA Signatures and RSA-PSS}
\label{subsec:rsa-sig}

The RSA trapdoor function lends itself naturally to signatures: because only the private-key holder can compute $d$-th powers modulo~$n$, a modular exponentiation with~$d$ serves as proof of identity.  The textbook RSA signature on message $m$ is $\sigma = H(m)^d \bmod n$, verified by checking $\sigma^e \equiv H(m) \pmod{n}$.  As with RSA encryption, the textbook version is insecure due to malleability.  The standard secure variant is \emph{RSA-PSS}\index{RSA-PSS} (Probabilistic Signature Scheme), which incorporates randomised padding and is provably EUF-CMA secure in the random oracle model under the RSA assumption.

\subsection{Schnorr Signatures and the Fiat--Shamir Heuristic}
\label{subsec:schnorr}

RSA-PSS derives its security from the hardness of factoring.  An entirely different design path begins not with a trapdoor function but with an \emph{identification protocol}---an interactive proof that one knows a secret---and then compiles it into a signature scheme.  Schnorr signatures\index{Schnorr signature} are the cleanest example of this approach, and the construction paradigm they introduce---the Fiat--Shamir heuristic---recurs throughout post-quantum cryptography.

\begin{definition}[Schnorr Signature]
\label{def:schnorr}
Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be a group of prime order~$q$ in which the discrete logarithm is hard.  The signer's key pair is $(pk, sk) = (g^x, x)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sign:} choose $r \getsr \Z_q$; compute $R = g^r$, $e = H(R \| m)$, and $s = r - ex \bmod q$.  Output $\sigma = (e, s)$.
\item \textbf{Verify:} compute $R' = g^s \cdot pk^e$ and accept if $H(R' \| m) = e$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Schnorr signatures are derived from a three-move identification protocol (commit~$R$, challenge~$e$, respond~$s$) via the \emph{Fiat--Shamir heuristic}\index{Fiat--Shamir heuristic}~\cite{FiatShamir1986}: the verifier's random challenge is replaced by the hash $H(R \| m)$.  This transform converts any honest-verifier zero-knowledge proof of knowledge of a discrete logarithm into a non-interactive signature scheme.  The Fiat--Shamir heuristic will appear again in the construction of lattice-based signatures (Chapter~\ref{ch:lattice-signatures}).

\subsection{ECDSA}
\label{subsec:ecdsa}

Just as elliptic curves shrank Diffie--Hellman key sizes by an order of magnitude, they do the same for signatures.  ECDSA combines the Schnorr-style ``commit-challenge-respond'' structure with the compact group arithmetic of elliptic curves, producing signatures that are both shorter and faster to verify than their RSA counterparts.

\begin{definition}[ECDSA]
\label{def:ecdsa}
\index{ECDSA}
Let $E(\F_p)$ be an elliptic curve with base point $G$ of order~$n$.  The signer's key pair is $(pk, sk) = (Q = dG,\; d)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Sign:} choose ephemeral $k \getsr \{1, \ldots, n-1\}$; compute $(x_1, y_1) = kG$; set $r = x_1 \bmod n$; compute $s = k^{-1}(H(m) + rd) \bmod n$.  Output $\sigma = (r, s)$.
\item \textbf{Verify:} compute $u_1 = H(m) s^{-1} \bmod n$, $u_2 = r s^{-1} \bmod n$; accept if the $x$-coordinate of $u_1 G + u_2 Q$ equals~$r \bmod n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

ECDSA is the workhorse signature algorithm in TLS~1.3, code signing, and cryptocurrency (Bitcoin, Ethereum).  A critical implementation requirement is that the ephemeral scalar $k$ must be generated with high-quality randomness: reuse of $k$ across two signatures reveals the private key~$d$ by simple algebra.  This vulnerability caused the 2010 PlayStation~3 private key extraction.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{Quantum vulnerability of signatures.}  Every signature scheme in this section---RSA-PSS, Schnorr, ECDSA---derives its security from the hardness of factoring or discrete logarithms.  Shor's algorithm breaks all of them.  Forging a signature allows an adversary to impersonate any server, sign malicious software as trusted vendors, or create fraudulent certificates.  Post-quantum signature schemes (Chapters~\ref{ch:lattice-signatures} and~\ref{ch:hash-signatures}) must preserve the EUF-CMA guarantee using quantum-resistant assumptions.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{Public Key Infrastructure}
\label{sec:pki}

Public-key cryptography solves key distribution but introduces a new problem: how does one verify that a given public key genuinely belongs to its claimed owner?  \emph{Public Key Infrastructure}\index{PKI} (PKI) answers this question at internet scale.

\subsection{Certificate Authorities and Chains of Trust}
\label{subsec:ca}

The answer is delegation: a small number of universally trusted entities vouch for the keys of others, who in turn vouch for still others.  PKI organises this delegation hierarchically\index{certificate authority}.  A small number of \emph{root Certificate Authorities} (CAs) are pre-installed in every browser and operating system.  Root CAs sign the certificates of \emph{intermediate CAs}, which in turn sign \emph{end-entity certificates} for individual domains.  This produces a \emph{certificate chain}:
\[
\text{Root CA} \;\xrightarrow{\text{signs}}\; \text{Intermediate CA} \;\xrightarrow{\text{signs}}\; \text{End-entity (e.g., github.com)}.
\]
Verification proceeds bottom-up: the relying party checks each signature in the chain until reaching a root that it trusts.

\subsection{X.509 Certificates}
\label{subsec:x509}

A chain of trust is only as precise as the documents it links.  The standard format for these documents is the X.509 certificate, whose fields have been refined over three decades to encode exactly the information a relying party needs to decide whether to trust a public key.

\begin{definition}[X.509 Certificate]
\label{def:x509}
\index{X.509}
An X.509 certificate binds a public key to an identity.  The \emph{Subject} field names the entity being certified (e.g., \texttt{CN=github.com}), while the \emph{Issuer} field identifies the CA that vouches for this binding---together, they answer \emph{who} holds the key and \emph{who} says so.  The \emph{Public key} field contains the subject's actual cryptographic key (RSA, ECDSA, or another algorithm), which is the whole point of the certificate: to distribute this key authentically.  The \emph{Validity period} limits the window of trust by specifying start and expiry dates, ensuring that compromised or outdated keys eventually expire even if revocation mechanisms fail.  The \emph{Signature} field carries the issuer's digital signature over all other fields, making any tampering detectable---this is the cryptographic glue that holds the chain together.  Finally, the \emph{Extensions} encode policy constraints: whether the key may be used for signing, encryption, or both; alternative names under which the subject operates; and whether the certificate may itself issue further certificates.  Extensions transform X.509 from a simple key--identity binding into a flexible policy language for large-scale trust management.
\end{definition}

Certificate chain validation verifies, for each certificate $C_i$ in the chain: (i)~the current time falls within the validity period; (ii)~$C_{i+1}$ has signed $C_i$; (iii)~$C_i$ has not been revoked (checked via OCSP or CRL); and (iv)~key-usage extensions permit the intended operation.  The chain terminates at a self-signed root certificate present in the local trust store.

\subsection{Failures and Lessons}
\label{subsec:pki-failures}

The X.509 model is elegant in theory, but it places extraordinary trust in certificate authorities---a trust that has, on occasion, been catastrophically misplaced.

\begin{example}[DigiNotar Collapse, 2011]
\label{ex:diginotar}
\index{DigiNotar}
Iranian attackers compromised the Dutch CA DigiNotar and issued fraudulent certificates for \texttt{*.google.com}, enabling man-in-the-middle surveillance of Iranian citizens.  DigiNotar declared bankruptcy within weeks; the Dutch government, whose sites relied on DigiNotar certificates, faced a crisis.  The incident accelerated the deployment of \emph{Certificate Transparency}\index{Certificate Transparency} logs, which provide a publicly auditable record of all issued certificates.
\end{example}

PKI currently secures over 200 million active certificates serving more than 4 billion users.  Its entire trust hierarchy depends on digital signatures (RSA or ECDSA).  When a quantum computer can forge these signatures, any adversary can fabricate certificates for arbitrary domains, impersonate certificate authorities, and collapse the chain of trust entirely.

\section{The TLS Protocol}
\label{sec:tls}

Transport Layer Security\index{TLS} (TLS) weaves together every primitive discussed so far---symmetric encryption, hash functions, digital signatures, key exchange, and certificates---into a single protocol that secures virtually all internet communications.  TLS~1.3 (RFC~8446, 2018) is the current standard.

\subsection{The TLS~1.3 Handshake}
\label{subsec:tls-handshake}

The protocol achieves its security guarantees through a carefully choreographed exchange called the \emph{handshake}, which establishes a shared symmetric key, authenticates the server, and protects against downgrade and replay attacks---all within a single network round trip.  A full TLS~1.3 handshake (1-RTT)\index{TLS!1-RTT handshake} proceeds as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{ClientHello (Client $\to$ Server).}  The client sends supported cipher suites, a random nonce, and---crucially---an ECDHE\index{ECDHE} key share (e.g., its X25519 public key).

\item \textbf{ServerHello (Server $\to$ Client).}  The server selects a cipher suite and responds with its own ECDHE key share and random nonce.  At this point, both sides can compute the ECDHE shared secret.

\item \textbf{Key derivation.}  Both sides derive multiple traffic secrets from the shared secret using HKDF\index{HKDF}:
\begin{align}
\text{Handshake Secret} &= \text{HKDF-Extract}(\text{ECDHE shared secret}), \label{eq:hkdf1}\\
\text{Traffic keys} &= \text{HKDF-Expand}(\text{Handshake Secret},\;\text{transcript hash}). \label{eq:hkdf2}
\end{align}
From this point, all subsequent handshake messages are encrypted.

\item \textbf{Certificate (Server $\to$ Client, encrypted).}  The server sends its certificate chain.  Unlike TLS~1.2, the certificate is transmitted under encryption, preventing passive observers from identifying the server.

\item \textbf{CertificateVerify (Server $\to$ Client, encrypted).}  The server signs the handshake transcript with its private key, proving possession of the key certified in the previous step.  The signature covers all negotiated parameters, preventing downgrade and replay attacks.

\item \textbf{Finished messages (both directions, encrypted).}  Each side sends an HMAC over the complete handshake transcript, providing key confirmation.

\item \textbf{Application data.}  Bulk data is encrypted with AES-256-GCM (or ChaCha20-Poly1305) using the derived application traffic keys.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cipher Suites}
\label{subsec:cipher-suites}

The handshake begins with a negotiation: client and server must agree on which algorithms to use.  TLS~1.3 dramatically simplified this step compared to its predecessors.  Only five AEAD cipher suites are defined; the most common is:

\begin{center}
\texttt{TLS\_AES\_256\_GCM\_SHA384}
\end{center}

\noindent which specifies AES-256-GCM for encryption and SHA-384 for the HKDF-based key schedule.  The key exchange algorithm (e.g., X25519 or P-256) and signature algorithm (e.g., ECDSA or RSA-PSS) are negotiated separately through extensions.

\label{subsec:tls-primitives}

With the handshake and cipher suites in place, we can now map every cryptographic primitive from this chapter to its role in TLS---and, critically, assess which components fall to quantum attack.  Table~\ref{tab:tls-primitives} provides this summary.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The post-quantum migration challenge.}
Replacing TLS's asymmetric components with post-quantum alternatives is not merely a cryptographic exercise---it is a coordination problem of enormous scale.  Every browser, server, operating system, and IoT device must be updated.  Post-quantum key encapsulation mechanisms (e.g., ML-KEM, Chapter~\ref{ch:lwe-encryption}) produce larger public keys and ciphertexts, increasing handshake sizes from~$\sim$4\,KB to~$\sim$15\,KB.  At the scale of 300~billion daily HTTPS connections, these increases have significant bandwidth implications.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{The Computational Hardness Foundation}
\label{sec:hardness}

The entire public-key infrastructure described above---RSA, Diffie--Hellman, ECDSA, TLS, PKI---rests on exactly three computational hardness assumptions\index{computational hardness}.  This mathematical monoculture is both the field's greatest success and its most critical vulnerability.

\subsection{The Integer Factorisation Problem}
\label{subsec:ifp}

We begin with the assumption that underpins RSA.  The security argument is simple: anyone can multiply two primes, but no one knows how to efficiently reverse the operation.

\begin{definition}[Integer Factorisation Problem (IFP)]
\label{def:ifp}
\index{integer factorisation problem}
Given a composite integer $n = pq$, where $p$ and $q$ are large primes, find the factors $p$ and $q$.
\end{definition}

The asymmetry is dramatic: multiplication $n = pq$ runs in $\bigO((\log n)^2)$ time, while the best classical factoring algorithm---the \emph{General Number Field Sieve}\index{General Number Field Sieve} (GNFS)---runs in sub-exponential time:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gnfs}
T_{\mathrm{GNFS}}(n) = \exp\!\left(c\,(\log n)^{1/3}\,(\log\log n)^{2/3}\right), \qquad c \approx 1.923.
\end{equation}
For RSA-2048, this amounts to roughly $2^{112}$ operations---well beyond reach.

The security of RSA relies on a slightly stronger assumption: not merely that factoring~$n$ is hard, but that computing $e$-th roots modulo $n$ (the \emph{RSA problem}\index{RSA problem}) is hard.  It is known that factoring reduces to the RSA problem, but the converse is open.

\subsection{The Discrete Logarithm Problem}
\label{subsec:dlp}

Where RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring, Diffie--Hellman and DSA rely on a different one-way phenomenon: modular exponentiation is easy, but extracting the exponent---computing a discrete logarithm---appears to be hard.

\begin{definition}[Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP)]
\label{def:dlp}
\index{discrete logarithm problem}
Given a cyclic group $G = \langle g \rangle$ of order~$q$ and an element $h \in G$, find the integer $x \in \{0, \ldots, q-1\}$ such that $g^x = h$.
\end{definition}

In the multiplicative group $\Z_p^*$, forward exponentiation ($g^x \bmod p$) costs $\bigO(\log x)$ multiplications, while the best classical discrete-log algorithm (index calculus) is sub-exponential.  For groups of prime order embedded in $\Z_p^*$, the DLP underpins the Diffie--Hellman protocol and DSA.

Knowing that discrete logarithms are hard does not, by itself, suffice for proving protocols secure.  The DLP says that recovering $a$ from $g^a$ is infeasible, but Diffie--Hellman key exchange never asks an adversary to find $a$---it asks whether the adversary can compute $g^{ab}$ from the pair $(g^a, g^b)$.  This is a potentially easier task: perhaps $g^{ab}$ can be computed without finding either $a$ or $b$.  Conversely, some protocols require an even stronger guarantee: not just that $g^{ab}$ cannot be \emph{computed}, but that it cannot even be \emph{distinguished} from a random group element.  These operational differences motivate two refinements of the DLP.

\begin{definition}[CDH and DDH Assumptions]
\label{def:cdh-ddh}
\index{CDH assumption}\index{DDH assumption}
Let $G = \langle g \rangle$ be a cyclic group of prime order~$q$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Computational Diffie--Hellman (CDH):}  Given $(g, g^a, g^b)$ for random $a, b \getsr \Z_q$, it is infeasible to compute $g^{ab}$.
\item \textbf{Decisional Diffie--Hellman (DDH):}  The distributions $(g^a, g^b, g^{ab})$ and $(g^a, g^b, g^c)$ are computationally indistinguishable for random $a, b, c \getsr \Z_q$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

These assumptions form a strict hierarchy: if one can solve the DLP, one can certainly compute $g^{ab}$ (just recover $a$ and exponentiate), so DLP~$\Rightarrow$~CDH.  Similarly, if one can compute $g^{ab}$, one can distinguish it from a random element, so CDH~$\Rightarrow$~DDH.  The converses are not known to hold, meaning DDH is the weakest---and therefore most conservative---assumption of the three.  In practice, the security of Diffie--Hellman key exchange relies on CDH (the adversary must compute the shared secret), while the semantic security of ElGamal encryption relies on DDH (the adversary must not even learn partial information about the plaintext).

\subsection{The Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem}
\label{subsec:ecdlp-hardness}

The DLP, CDH, and DDH can be stated in any cyclic group.  When the group is $\Z_p^*$, sub-exponential index-calculus algorithms exist, forcing the use of multi-thousand-bit primes.  Elliptic curve groups resist these algorithms entirely, which is why they deliver equivalent security at a fraction of the key size.  The formal hardness assumption is the natural analogue of the DLP, transplanted to the curve.

\begin{definition}[Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP)]
\label{def:ecdlp}
\index{ECDLP}
Given an elliptic curve $E$ over $\F_p$, a base point $G \in E(\F_p)$ of order~$n$, and a point $P = kG$, find the integer $k$.
\end{definition}

The best classical algorithm for ECDLP is Pollard's rho method\index{Pollard's rho}, which runs in $\bigO(\sqrt{n})$ time---fully exponential in the key length.  There is no analogue of index calculus for generic elliptic curves, which is why 256-bit ECC achieves 128-bit security whereas 3072-bit RSA is needed for the same level (Table~\ref{tab:key-sizes}).  This efficiency advantage has made ECC the default for internet-scale cryptography.

\subsection{The Monoculture Risk}
\label{subsec:monoculture}

Three problems, three different-looking mathematical structures---yet beneath the surface, they are uncomfortably alike.  All three---IFP, DLP, ECDLP---share a deep structural property: they reduce to instances of the \emph{hidden subgroup problem}\index{hidden subgroup problem} (HSP) over abelian groups.  Shor's algorithm\index{Shor's algorithm} solves the HSP in polynomial time on a quantum computer~\cite{Shor1997}, which means:

\begin{theorem}[Quantum Collapse of Classical Assumptions]
\label{thm:quantum-collapse}
A sufficiently large quantum computer running Shor's algorithm solves:
\begin{enumerate}
\item IFP (factoring an $n$-bit integer) in $\bigO(n^3)$ quantum gates.
\item DLP in $\Z_p^*$ in $\bigO((\log p)^3)$ quantum gates.
\item ECDLP over $E(\F_p)$ in $\bigO((\log p)^3)$ quantum gates.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The mathematical monoculture means that a single algorithmic advance---Shor's algorithm---simultaneously demolishes factoring, discrete logarithms, and elliptic curve discrete logarithms.  Every protocol examined in this chapter collapses: RSA encryption and signatures, Diffie--Hellman key exchange, ECDSA, the certificate chains of PKI, and the asymmetric core of TLS.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{Why ``bigger keys'' cannot help.}
Against classical attacks, doubling the RSA modulus roughly squares the attack cost; this scaling has kept RSA viable for decades.  Against Shor's algorithm, the attack cost grows only polynomially with key size.  No feasible key length can bridge an exponential-to-polynomial collapse.  The only path forward is to replace the underlying mathematical problems with ones that resist quantum attack---the subject of the remainder of this book.
\end{svgraybox}
