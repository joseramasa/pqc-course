\section{Definitions and Basic Properties}
\label{sec:lattice-defs}

In crystallography, a lattice\index{lattice} describes the periodic arrangement of atoms in a crystal---a discrete set of points with translational symmetry.  Cryptographic lattices are the same mathematical object, but embedded in hundreds of dimensions where geometric intuition breaks down and computational problems become exponentially hard~\cite{MicciancioGoldwasser2002}.

\begin{definition}[Lattice]
\label{def:lattice}
A \emph{lattice}\index{lattice!definition} $\lat \subseteq \R^n$ is the set of all integer linear combinations of $n$ linearly independent vectors $\mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_n \in \R^n$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lattice-def}
\lat(\mathbf{B}) \;=\; \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \,\mathbf{b}_i \;:\; a_i \in \Z \right\} \;=\; \bigl\{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x} \in \Z^n\bigr\},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{B} = [\mathbf{b}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \mathbf{b}_n] \in \R^{n \times n}$ is the \emph{basis matrix}\index{lattice!basis}.  The integer $n$ is the \emph{rank} (or \emph{dimension}) of the lattice.
\end{definition}

A lattice admits infinitely many bases: if $\mathbf{B}$ is a basis and $\mathbf{U}\in\Z^{n\times n}$ is any unimodular matrix\index{unimodular matrix} ($\det\mathbf{U}=\pm 1$), then $\mathbf{B}' = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}$ generates the same lattice.  This non-uniqueness of the basis is the source of cryptographic hardness, as we shall see in Sect.~\ref{sec:lattice-problems}.

\begin{example}[From NaCl to Cryptography]
\label{ex:nacl}
The face-centred cubic lattice of NaCl lives in $\R^3$.  Finding the nearest atom to any point in space is a straightforward computation.  Now imagine the same lattice embedded in $\R^{700}$: finding nearest points becomes exponentially hard.  This dimension-induced hardness\index{lattice!dimension hardness} is the foundation of lattice-based cryptography.
\end{example}

The correspondence between crystallography\index{crystallography} and lattice-based cryptography runs deeper than terminology.  In a crystal, the \emph{lattice points} mark the positions of identical atoms or molecules---the discrete translational symmetry that produces sharp Bragg peaks in X-ray diffraction\index{Bragg diffraction}.  The \emph{unit cell}\index{unit cell} is the fundamental parallelotope\index{parallelotope}: the smallest region that tiles all of space under lattice translations, exactly the object we formalise below as the fundamental parallelepiped.  Most strikingly, the \emph{reciprocal lattice}\index{reciprocal lattice} of crystallography---whose points index the spatial frequencies of the electron density---is the Fourier dual of the direct lattice, and in the cryptographic setting it reappears as the dual lattice (Sect.~\ref{subsec:dual-lattice}), with identical algebraic properties and analogous hardness guarantees.

The algorithmic parallels are equally instructive.  Lattice parameter refinement in crystallography---adjusting unit-cell dimensions to fit observed diffraction data---mirrors the basis reduction algorithms of Sect.~\ref{sec:basis-reduction}.  Peak finding in a diffraction pattern amounts to locating short vectors in the reciprocal lattice, a low-dimensional cousin of the Shortest Vector Problem.  The crystallographic phase problem\index{phase problem}---recovering electron density from diffraction intensities when phase information is lost---connects to hidden subgroup problems in the same way that Shor's algorithm connects to period finding.  The difference, always, is dimension: in three dimensions these problems have elegant polynomial-time solutions; in five hundred dimensions, they become the intractable foundations of modern cryptography.


\subsection{Determinant and Volume}
\label{subsec:determinant}

If a lattice can be described by many different bases, we need a quantity that is intrinsic to the lattice itself---something that does not change when we swap one basis for another.  The determinant provides exactly this invariant: it captures how densely the lattice points pack the ambient space.

\begin{definition}[Lattice Determinant]
\label{def:determinant}
The \emph{determinant}\index{lattice!determinant} of a lattice $\lat(\mathbf{B})$ is
\begin{equation}
\det(\lat) \;=\; \abs{\det(\mathbf{B})}.
\end{equation}
It equals the $n$-dimensional volume of the \emph{fundamental parallelepiped}\index{fundamental parallelepiped}
$\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{B}) = \bigl\{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{x} : \mathbf{x}\in[0,1)^n\bigr\}$
and is independent of the choice of basis.
\end{definition}

Geometrically, $\det(\lat)$ measures the ``density'' of lattice points: the larger the determinant, the sparser the lattice.  In a crystal, this corresponds to the volume of the unit cell.


\subsection{Successive Minima}
\label{subsec:successive-minima}

The determinant tells us about global density, but cryptography demands something more specific: how short can lattice vectors actually be?  The answer determines the security margin of every lattice-based scheme, because an attacker who can find short vectors can break keys.  The successive minima formalise this question by measuring, for each dimension, the radius at which a new linearly independent lattice vector first appears.

\begin{definition}[Successive Minima]
\label{def:successive-minima}
The $i$-th \emph{successive minimum}\index{successive minima} $\lambda_i(\lat)$ of a lattice $\lat$ is the smallest radius $r$ such that the closed ball $\overline{B}(\mathbf{0}, r)$ contains $i$ linearly independent lattice vectors:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_i(\lat) \;=\; \inf\bigl\{r > 0 : \dim\bigl(\operatorname{span}(\lat \cap \overline{B}(\mathbf{0},r))\bigr) \geq i\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
In particular, $\lambda_1(\lat)$ is the length of a shortest non-zero lattice vector.
\end{definition}

The successive minima are related to the determinant by \emph{Minkowski's theorem}\index{Minkowski's theorem}:
\begin{theorem}[Minkowski's First Theorem]
\label{thm:minkowski}
For any $n$-dimensional lattice~$\lat$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:minkowski}
\lambda_1(\lat) \;\leq\; \sqrt{n}\;\det(\lat)^{1/n}.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

For random lattices, the bound is essentially tight: the \emph{Gaussian heuristic}\index{Gaussian heuristic} predicts $\lambda_1(\lat) \approx \sqrt{n/(2\pi e)}\;\det(\lat)^{1/n}$.


\subsection{The Dual Lattice}
\label{subsec:dual-lattice}

Every lattice carries a shadow: a companion lattice whose points have integer inner products with all points of the original.  Physicists know this construction as the reciprocal lattice, the object that governs diffraction patterns and Brillouin zones.  In cryptography, the dual lattice matters because hardness results transfer across the duality---an adversary gains nothing by attacking the dual instead of the original.

\begin{definition}[Dual Lattice]
\label{def:dual-lattice}
The \emph{dual}\index{lattice!dual} (or \emph{reciprocal}) lattice of $\lat$ is
\begin{equation}
\lat^* \;=\; \bigl\{\mathbf{y}\in\R^n : \ip{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}} \in \Z \;\;\text{for all}\;\; \mathbf{x}\in\lat\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
If $\lat = \lat(\mathbf{B})$, then $\lat^* = \lat((\mathbf{B}^{-1})^\top)$.
\end{definition}

The key properties are $\det(\lat^*) = 1/\det(\lat)$ and $\lambda_1(\lat)\cdot\lambda_n(\lat^*) \leq n$ (the \emph{transference theorem}\index{transference theorem}).  The transference theorem makes the cryptographic consequence precise: short vectors in the primal lattice imply long vectors in the dual, and vice versa, so neither perspective offers an easier attack surface.


\subsection{Good Bases vs.\ Bad Bases}
\label{subsec:good-bad-basis}

We have defined lattices, their determinants, their shortest vectors, and their duals.  All of these are properties of the lattice itself, independent of how it is presented.  But the \emph{presentation} matters enormously in practice: the same lattice can be described by bases of vastly different quality, and this ambiguity is precisely what makes cryptography possible.  A ``good'' basis reveals the lattice's short vectors at a glance; a ``bad'' basis buries them under layers of near-cancellation.  The private key is the good basis; the public key is the bad one\index{lattice!basis quality}.

\begin{example}[Concrete Basis Comparison]
\label{ex:basis-comparison}
Consider the 2D integer lattice $\Z^2$ with two bases:

\noindent\textbf{Good basis:} $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{good}} = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$.
The shortest vector $(1,0)$ has length~$1$ and is read off immediately.

\noindent\textbf{Bad basis:} $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{bad}} = \bigl(\begin{smallmatrix} 97 & 89 \\ 89 & 81 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$.
This generates the same lattice ($\det = 97\cdot 81 - 89\cdot 89 = 1$), but the basis vectors have length $> 100$.  The shortest vector is still $(1,0)$, which equals $97\mathbf{b}_1 - 89\mathbf{b}_2$---a non-trivial integer combination.
\end{example}

To make the notion of ``good'' and ``bad'' precise, we need quantitative measures.  Two are standard: one captures how far the basis vectors are from orthogonal, and the other measures how well the first basis vector approximates the true shortest vector.

\begin{definition}[Basis Quality Measures]
\label{def:basis-quality}
For a basis $\mathbf{B} = [\mathbf{b}_1 \mid \cdots \mid \mathbf{b}_n]$, define:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Orthogonality defect\index{orthogonality defect}:}
  $\delta(\mathbf{B}) = \frac{\prod_{i=1}^n \norm{\mathbf{b}_i}}{\det(\lat)}$
  \quad(equals~$1$ for an orthogonal basis).
\item \textbf{Hermite factor\index{Hermite factor}:}
  $\gamma = \bigl(\frac{\norm{\mathbf{b}_1}}{\det(\lat)^{1/n}}\bigr)^{1/n}$
  \quad(smaller is better; measures how well $\mathbf{b}_1$ approximates $\lambda_1$).
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}


\subsection{High-Dimensional Phenomena}
\label{subsec:high-dim}

Everything we have discussed so far---the determinant, the successive minima, the gap between good and bad bases---becomes qualitatively different when the dimension $n$ grows from the single digits familiar to crystallographers to the hundreds required by cryptographers.  High-dimensional Euclidean space is profoundly counter-intuitive, and these counter-intuitions are what make lattice problems hard.

\begin{theorem}[Dimension Changes Everything]
\label{thm:high-dim}
In dimension~$n$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The volume of the unit ball $V_n = \pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2+1)$ converges to~$0$ as $n\to\infty$.
\item Almost all the volume concentrates near the surface: $V_n(r)/V_n(R) = (r/R)^n$.
\item Random unit vectors are nearly orthogonal: $\EE[\abs{\ip{\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{y}}}] = \bigO(1/\sqrt{n})$.
\item The number of lattice vectors of length close to $\lambda_1$ grows as $\exp(\Theta(n))$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The contrast with low-dimensional physics is worth stating explicitly, because it is the single most important fact in this chapter.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The dimension phase transition.}
In condensed-matter physics, lattice problems in 3D are polynomial-time computations---band structures, ground-state energies, defect calculations.  In 500 dimensions, the same problems become exponentially hard.  This dimension-induced phase transition from tractable to intractable is what protects cryptographic secrets.
\end{svgraybox}

The following table illustrates the concrete impact of dimension on the hardness of the shortest vector problem:

\textbf{Dimension} & \textbf{Time (best known)} & \textbf{Feasible?} \\

With these geometric foundations in place---definitions, determinants, successive minima, duality, and the high-dimensional phenomena that make everything hard---we can now state the computational problems that lattice-based cryptography actually relies on.

\section{Fundamental Computational Problems}
\label{sec:lattice-problems}

The geometry of high-dimensional lattices would be a curiosity if it did not give rise to computational problems of extraordinary hardness.  Two problems in particular---finding short vectors and finding close vectors---form the foundation of lattice-based cryptography\index{lattice!computational problems}.  Unlike integer factorisation or the discrete logarithm, they have resisted efficient algorithms for decades, classical and quantum alike.  Everything in the next four chapters ultimately reduces to the hardness of one of these two problems.

\subsection{The Shortest Vector Problem}
\label{subsec:svp}

The most natural question one can ask about a lattice is also the hardest: given a basis, find the shortest non-zero vector.  In low dimensions this is straightforward---geometrically obvious, even.  In hundreds of dimensions, it becomes a needle-in-a-haystack problem where the haystack has exponentially many straws of nearly equal length.

\begin{definition}[Shortest Vector Problem (\svp{})]
\label{def:svp}
\index{SVP}\index{shortest vector problem|see{SVP}}
Given a basis $\mathbf{B}$ of a lattice $\lat$, find a non-zero vector $\mathbf{v}\in\lat$ of minimum Euclidean norm:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v} \in \lat\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\} \quad\text{such that}\quad \norm{\mathbf{v}} = \lambda_1(\lat).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Why is \svp{} hard?
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exponentially many candidates:} a ball of radius $R$ contains approximately $R^n / \det(\lat)$ lattice points.
\item \textbf{No local structure:} gradient-descent methods cannot navigate a discrete set.
\item \textbf{Basis dependence:} a good basis makes the problem trivial; a bad basis makes it exponentially hard.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch06-svp} visualises the problem: the green vector is the shortest non-zero lattice vector $\lambda_1$, and the dashed circle marks the minimum distance from the origin.

\subsection{The Closest Vector Problem}
\label{subsec:cvp}

The Shortest Vector Problem asks us to find structure \emph{within} the lattice.  The Closest Vector Problem asks something subtly different: given a point that is \emph{not} on the lattice, find the lattice point nearest to it.  This is the decoding problem in disguise, and it will become the beating heart of Learning With Errors.

\begin{definition}[Closest Vector Problem (\cvp{})]
\label{def:cvp}
\index{CVP}\index{closest vector problem|see{CVP}}
Given a basis $\mathbf{B}$ of a lattice $\lat$ and a target point $\mathbf{t}\in\R^n$, find a lattice vector closest to $\mathbf{t}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{v} \in \lat \quad\text{such that}\quad \norm{\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{t}} = \min_{\mathbf{x}\in\lat}\norm{\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{t}}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\cvp{} is the \emph{decoding problem}\index{decoding problem}: given a noisy observation $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{e}$ (lattice point plus small error), recover $\mathbf{v}$.  This connection to decoding will reappear in Chap.~\ref{ch:lwe-encryption}, where the Learning With Errors problem is essentially a structured instance of \cvp{}.

\begin{theorem}[\svp{}--\cvp{} Connection]
\label{thm:svp-cvp}
The two problems are intimately related~\cite{MicciancioGoldwasser2002}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Any $\gamma$-\cvp{} oracle yields a $(2\gamma)$-\svp{} algorithm.
\item Both problems have the same quantum complexity up to polynomial factors.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}


\subsection{Approximation Variants}
\label{subsec:approx-variants}

Exact \svp{} and \cvp{} are NP-hard, but cryptography does not need exact solutions---and neither do attackers.  What matters is how \emph{close} to exact one must get before the problem becomes tractable.  This is controlled by the approximation factor~$\gamma$\index{approximation factor}, and the relationship between $\gamma$ and computational complexity is where lattice-based security parameters come from.

\begin{definition}[Approximate \svp{} and \cvp{}]
\label{def:approx-svp-cvp}
\index{SVP!approximate}\index{CVP!approximate}
\begin{align}
\gamma\text{-\svp{}:}\quad & \text{Find } \mathbf{v}\in\lat\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\} \text{ with } \norm{\mathbf{v}} \leq \gamma\cdot\lambda_1(\lat). \\
\gamma\text{-\cvp{}:}\quad & \text{Find } \mathbf{v}\in\lat \text{ with } \norm{\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{t}} \leq \gamma\cdot\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{t},\lat).
\end{align}
\end{definition}

The complexity landscape depends critically on~$\gamma$:

\subsection{Additional Problems}
\label{subsec:additional-problems}

\svp{} and \cvp{} are the problems an attacker must solve to break a scheme.  But security \emph{proofs} often reduce from different problems---ones whose worst-case hardness is easier to characterise.  Two such problems recur throughout the literature.

The first generalises \svp{} from finding one short vector to finding an entire basis of short vectors:

\begin{definition}[Shortest Independent Vectors Problem (\sivp{})]
\label{def:sivp}
\index{SIVP}
Given a basis of an $n$-dimensional lattice~$\lat$, find $n$ linearly independent vectors $\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n \in \lat$ with $\max_i \norm{\mathbf{v}_i} \leq \gamma\cdot\lambda_n(\lat)$.
\end{definition}

The second converts the search problem into a decision problem---not ``find a short vector'' but ``is there a short vector?''  This formulation connects more naturally to the complexity classes NP and coNP:

\begin{definition}[Decisional GapSVP (\gapsvp{})]
\label{def:gapsvp}
\index{GapSVP}
Given a basis of $\lat$ and a value $d$, distinguish between $\lambda_1(\lat)\leq d$ and $\lambda_1(\lat) > \gamma\cdot d$.
\end{definition}

\gapsvp{} is the decision version most commonly used in security reductions to \lwe{} (see Chap.~\ref{ch:lwe-encryption}).  It is known to lie in $\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}$ for $\gamma = \sqrt{n}$~\cite{Ajtai1996, MicciancioGoldwasser2002}.

We have now catalogued the problems.  The next question is practical: how well can algorithms actually solve them?  The answer comes from basis reduction---the art of transforming a bad basis into a better one---and it determines every security parameter in lattice-based cryptography.

\section{Lattice Basis Reduction}
\label{sec:basis-reduction}

The computational problems of the previous section are defined abstractly: ``find a short vector,'' ``find the closest lattice point.''  The primary algorithmic approach to all of them is the same---improve the basis until short vectors reveal themselves.  \emph{Basis reduction}\index{basis reduction} algorithms transform a bad basis into a better one, producing shorter and more orthogonal vectors.  They are both the main tool for \emph{solving} lattice problems and, consequently, the main tool for \emph{attacking} lattice-based cryptosystems.  Understanding their power---and its limits---is essential for setting secure parameters.

\subsection{Gram--Schmidt Orthogonalisation}
\label{subsec:gram-schmidt}

The quality of a basis is measured against its \emph{Gram--Schmidt orthogonalisation}\index{Gram--Schmidt orthogonalisation} (GSO).  Given $\mathbf{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{b}_n)$, define
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_i \;=\; \mathbf{b}_i - \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \mu_{i,j}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_j, \qquad \mu_{i,j} = \frac{\ip{\mathbf{b}_i}{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_j}}{\ip{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_j}{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_j}}.
\end{equation}
The vectors $\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_i$ are orthogonal but in general \emph{not} lattice vectors.  A well-reduced basis is one where the $\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_i}$ decrease slowly---that is, no GSO vector is much shorter than its predecessor.


\subsection{The LLL Algorithm}
\label{subsec:lll}

The Lenstra--Lenstra--Lov\'asz (LLL) algorithm\index{LLL algorithm}, published in~1982~\cite{LLL1982}, was the first polynomial-time lattice reduction algorithm.  It remains the standard first step in any lattice attack.

What does ``reduced'' mean precisely?  LLL defines two conditions on a basis: the Gram--Schmidt coefficients must be small (the basis is nearly size-reduced), and consecutive GSO vectors must not drop too sharply in norm (the Lov\'asz condition).  Together, these prevent the pathological near-cancellations that make bad bases bad.

\begin{definition}[LLL-Reduced Basis]
\label{def:lll-reduced}
A basis $(\mathbf{b}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{b}_n)$ is \emph{LLL-reduced}\index{LLL algorithm!reduced basis} with parameter $\delta\in(1/4,1]$ if:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Size-reduced:} $|\mu_{i,j}| \leq 1/2$ for all $1 \leq j < i \leq n$.
\item \textbf{Lov\'asz condition:} $\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_i}^2 \geq \bigl(\delta - \mu_{i,i-1}^2\bigr)\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_{i-1}}^2$ for all $2 \leq i \leq n$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{The LLL Algorithm}
\label{alg:lll}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Basis $\mathbf{B} = (\mathbf{b}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{b}_n)$, parameter $\delta\in(1/4,1]$
\Ensure LLL-reduced basis
\State Compute GSO $(\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_i, \mu_{i,j})$
\State $k \gets 2$
\While{$k \leq n$}
  \For{$j = k-1, k-2, \ldots, 1$} \Comment{Size reduction}
    \If{$|\mu_{k,j}| > 1/2$}
      \State $\mathbf{b}_k \gets \mathbf{b}_k - \lfloor \mu_{k,j}\rceil\,\mathbf{b}_j$
      \State Update $\mu$ values
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
  \If{$\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_k}^2 \geq (\delta - \mu_{k,k-1}^2)\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_{k-1}}^2$} \Comment{Lov\'asz condition}
    \State $k \gets k + 1$
  \Else
    \State Swap $\mathbf{b}_k \leftrightarrow \mathbf{b}_{k-1}$
    \State Update GSO
    \State $k \gets \max(k-1, 2)$
  \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State \Return $(\mathbf{b}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{b}_n)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[LLL Guarantees~\cite{LLL1982}]
\label{thm:lll-guarantees}
\index{LLL algorithm!guarantees}
The LLL algorithm with $\delta = 3/4$ runs in polynomial time $\bigO(n^5 d\log^3 B)$ (where $B$ bounds the basis vector norms) and produces a basis whose first vector satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lll-bound}
\norm{\mathbf{b}_1} \;\leq\; 2^{(n-1)/2}\,\lambda_1(\lat).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The exponential factor $2^{(n-1)/2}$ is the price of polynomial running time.  For $n = 500$, this bound is astronomically loose: LLL finds a vector that is at most $2^{250}$ times the shortest.  This is why LLL alone does \emph{not} break lattice-based cryptosystems, though it serves as a crucial preprocessing step.


\subsection{BKZ: The Practical Workhorse}
\label{subsec:bkz}

LLL finds short vectors in polynomial time, but its approximation quality degrades exponentially with dimension---far too loose to threaten cryptographic parameters.  To do better, we need to invest more computation.  The \emph{Block Korkin--Zolotarev} (BKZ) algorithm\index{BKZ algorithm} of Schnorr and Euchner~(1994) achieves this by applying exact SVP solvers to local blocks within the basis, then propagating the improvement globally.  The block size~$\beta$\index{BKZ algorithm!block size} controls the tradeoff: larger blocks yield shorter vectors but cost exponentially more time.  The core idea is simple enough to fit in a single paragraph:

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{BKZ-$\beta$ in a nutshell.}  Slide a window of width~$\beta$ along the GSO, solve exact \svp{} within each projected block, and insert the short vector back into the basis.  Repeat until no further progress.
\end{svgraybox}

\noindent The tradeoff is controlled entirely by $\beta$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\beta = 2$ recovers LLL.
\item $\beta = n$ recovers HKZ reduction (exact \svp{}, exponential time).
\item For intermediate $\beta$, BKZ-$\beta$ runs in time $T(\beta)\cdot\poly(n)$, where $T(\beta)$ is the cost of the SVP oracle in dimension $\beta$.
\end{itemize}

To quantify BKZ's output, we need a single number that summarises how short the first basis vector is relative to the lattice determinant.  The root Hermite factor serves this purpose---it is the parameter that practitioners use to translate block size into concrete security estimates.

\begin{definition}[Root Hermite Factor]
\label{def:root-hermite}
\index{root Hermite factor}
After BKZ-$\beta$ reduction, the first basis vector satisfies
\begin{equation}
\norm{\mathbf{b}_1} \;\approx\; \delta_0^n \cdot \det(\lat)^{1/n},
\end{equation}
where the \emph{root Hermite factor} $\delta_0$ depends on $\beta$ approximately as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:root-hermite}
\delta_0 \;\approx\; \left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi e}\bigl(\pi\beta\bigr)^{1/\beta}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(\beta-1)}}.
\end{equation}
Smaller $\delta_0$ means stronger reduction.
\end{definition}

In practice, BKZ-$\beta$ with $\beta \geq 60$ already surpasses what LLL can achieve, and the transition to the cryptographically relevant regime occurs around $\beta \approx 300$--$400$.


\subsection{The Core-SVP Hardness Model}
\label{subsec:core-svp}

The root Hermite factor tells us what block size an attacker needs.  To convert that block size into a security level measured in bits---the currency that NIST and every standards body uses---we need one more ingredient: the cost of the SVP oracle that BKZ calls as a subroutine.  The \emph{core-SVP model}\index{core-SVP model} makes this connection explicit.

\begin{definition}[Core-SVP Cost]
\label{def:core-svp}
The cost of running BKZ-$\beta$ is dominated by the cost of the SVP oracle in dimension~$\beta$.  Using the best known algorithms:
\begin{align}
\text{Classical sieving:}\quad & T_{\mathrm{class}}(\beta) = 2^{0.292\,\beta + o(\beta)}, \label{eq:classical-sieve}\\
\text{Quantum sieving:}\quad  & T_{\mathrm{quant}}(\beta) = 2^{0.265\,\beta + o(\beta)}. \label{eq:quantum-sieve}
\end{align}
\end{definition}

Given a lattice-based scheme with parameters requiring BKZ block size $\beta$ to break, the security in bits is $\approx 0.292\,\beta$ (classical) or $\approx 0.265\,\beta$ (quantum).  This model is used to set the parameters of ML-KEM and ML-DSA (Chaps.~\ref{ch:lwe-encryption}--\ref{ch:lattice-signatures}).

Basis reduction gives us a concrete understanding of what attackers can do.  But how confident should we be that these problems are \emph{truly} hard, rather than merely hard for current algorithms?  The next section addresses this question with a result that has no analogue in classical cryptography: a proof that average-case hardness follows from worst-case hardness.

\section{Worst-Case to Average-Case Reductions}
\label{sec:worst-avg-case}

Basis reduction algorithms tell us how hard lattice problems are \emph{in practice}---for the best algorithms we know today.  But cryptographic confidence demands something deeper.  How do we know that a clever new algorithm will not suddenly make random lattice instances easy, the way Shor's algorithm made random factoring instances easy?  Classical cryptography has no answer to this question: we \emph{believe} that random RSA moduli are hard to factor, but we cannot prove it.  Lattice-based cryptography offers something stronger.

\subsection{Why Average-Case Hardness Matters}
\label{subsec:avg-case-matters}

The distinction between worst-case and average-case hardness is subtle but consequential.  A cryptographic scheme generates its keys \emph{at random}.  Its security therefore depends on the hardness of random instances, not worst-case instances.  Knowing that some lattice instances are exponentially hard is cold comfort if the random instances used in practice happen to be easy.  A \emph{worst-case to average-case reduction}\index{worst-case to average-case reduction} eliminates this gap: it shows that if \emph{any} algorithm breaks a non-negligible fraction of random instances, then the same algorithm can be used to solve \emph{every} instance of a worst-case problem believed to be hard.  This is a qualitatively different kind of security guarantee---one that RSA, Diffie--Hellman, and elliptic-curve cryptography simply do not have.

\subsection{Ajtai's Breakthrough}
\label{subsec:ajtai}

In 1996, Mikl\'os Ajtai proved the result that made lattice-based cryptography possible~\cite{Ajtai1996}\index{Ajtai's reduction}.  He showed that a specific average-case problem---finding short integer solutions to a random system of linear equations modulo~$q$---is at least as hard as the worst case of~\sivp{}.  The problem itself is deceptively simple:

\begin{definition}[Short Integer Solutions (\textsc{SIS})]
\label{def:sis}
\index{SIS}
Given a uniformly random matrix $\mathbf{A}\in\Zq^{n\times m}$ and a bound $\beta$, find a non-zero vector $\mathbf{x}\in\Z^m$ with $\norm{\mathbf{x}}\leq\beta$ such that
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \pmod{q}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Ajtai~\cite{Ajtai1996}, Micciancio--Regev~\cite{MicciancioRegev2007}]
\label{thm:ajtai}
For appropriate parameters, solving \textsc{SIS}\index{SIS!hardness} on a random $\mathbf{A}$ is at least as hard as solving $\gamma$-\sivp{} on \emph{every} $n$-dimensional lattice (worst case), where $\gamma = \beta\cdot\poly(n)$.
\end{theorem}

The implication is remarkable: breaking a cryptosystem built on random \textsc{SIS} instances would require an algorithm capable of finding short vectors in \emph{every} lattice---including the hardest ones.  No other public-key family offers a comparable guarantee.


\subsection{Proof Intuition}
\label{subsec:reduction-intuition}

The reduction works by showing that a random matrix $\mathbf{A}$ encodes information about an arbitrary ``target'' lattice in a way that is invisible to the adversary.  The key tools are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Gaussian sampling\index{Gaussian sampling}:} Sampling lattice points from a discrete Gaussian distribution $D_{\lat,s}$ with standard deviation~$s$.  When $s$ is above the \emph{smoothing parameter}\index{smoothing parameter} $\eta_\varepsilon(\lat)$, the samples look statistically close to continuous Gaussians, hiding the lattice structure.

\item \textbf{The smoothing parameter:} $\eta_\varepsilon(\lat)$ is the smallest $s$ such that $D_{\lat^*,1/s}$ has mass at most $1+\varepsilon$ at the origin.  Intuitively, it is the scale at which the lattice ``looks smooth'' (continuous) to a statistical test.

\item \textbf{Rerandomisation:} By combining Gaussian samples from the target lattice with the \textsc{SIS} oracle's output, the reduction transforms the adversary's success into a \sivp{} solver.
\end{enumerate}

A complete proof appears in Appendix~\ref{app:ajtai}.  We note that Regev~\cite{Regev2009} later proved an analogous reduction for \lwe{} (Chap.~\ref{ch:lwe-encryption}), using a \emph{quantum} reduction from \gapsvp{} to \lwe{}.

Ajtai's result and its descendants give lattice-based cryptography a theoretical foundation unmatched in public-key cryptography.  But theoretical hardness reductions involve polynomial losses that can be large, and they say nothing about the \emph{exact} complexity of the problems involved.  The final section of this chapter surveys what we know---and what remains open---about the concrete complexity of lattice problems, both classically and quantumly.

\section{Computational Complexity of Lattice Problems}
\label{sec:lattice-complexity}

\subsection{Classical Complexity}
\label{subsec:classical-complexity}

We have seen that basis reduction algorithms provide upper bounds on how easy lattice problems are, and worst-case reductions provide lower bounds on how hard they must be.  The full complexity picture fills in the space between these bounds.  The results of three decades of study~\cite{MicciancioGoldwasser2002, Peikert2016} can be summarised as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Exact \svp{}:} NP-hard under randomised reductions~\cite{Ajtai1996}.
\item \textbf{$\gamma$-\gapsvp{} for $\gamma = \sqrt{n}$:} in $\mathrm{NP}\cap\mathrm{coNP}$ (hence unlikely to be NP-hard).
\item \textbf{$\gamma$-\svp{} for $\gamma = \poly(n)$:} solvable in polynomial time via LLL.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Quantum Complexity}
\label{subsec:quantum-complexity}

For a book on post-quantum cryptography, this subsection contains the most important single fact: quantum computers do not help much with lattice problems.  The contrast with factoring is stark.  Where Shor's algorithm obliterates RSA by exploiting algebraic periodicity, the best known quantum algorithms for lattice problems provide only a modest polynomial speedup---far too small to threaten properly sized parameters.

\begin{theorem}[Quantum Speedup for Lattice Problems]
\label{thm:quantum-lattice}
The best known quantum algorithms for \svp{} in dimension~$n$ achieve:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{quant}} \;=\; 2^{0.265n + o(n)},
\end{equation}
compared to the classical $2^{0.292n + o(n)}$.  The speedup factor is $2^{0.027n} \approx 1.019^n$---polynomial, not exponential.
\end{theorem}

This stands in sharp contrast to factoring, where Shor's algorithm provides an \emph{exponential} speedup (from sub-exponential $e^{\bigO(n^{1/3}\log^{2/3}n)}$ to polynomial $\bigO(n^3)$).  The natural question is: why?  What is it about lattice problems that denies quantum computers the leverage they enjoy elsewhere?

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{Why no quantum breakthrough for lattices?}
Four structural reasons are commonly cited:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{No periodic structure:} Shor's algorithm exploits the periodicity of modular exponentiation.  Lattice problems lack comparable periodic structure.
\item \emph{No hidden subgroup:} The best quantum algorithms for algebraic problems exploit the hidden subgroup framework.  Lattice problems do not fit naturally into this framework.
\item \emph{Geometric, not algebraic:} Lattice problems are fundamentally geometric.  Quantum computers have not shown exponential advantages for geometric optimisation.
\item \emph{Grover gives little:} The search space is too large for Grover's quadratic speedup to be meaningful ($2^{0.146n}$ is still exponential).
\end{enumerate}
\end{svgraybox}

The narrow gap between classical and quantum SVP curves---in contrast to the chasm between classical and quantum factoring---is the geometric reason that lattice-based cryptography survives the quantum transition.

\subsection{Summary: Why Lattices Survive Quantum Computers}
\label{subsec:lattice-summary}

We can now see why NIST chose lattice-based schemes as the primary post-quantum standards.  Lattice problems occupy a unique position in the complexity landscape, combining five properties that no other cryptographic family matches simultaneously.  Average-case hardness reduces to worst-case hardness~\cite{Ajtai1996, Regev2009}, so random instances are provably no easier than the hardest ones.  Exact \svp{} is NP-hard, anchoring the entire edifice to a complexity-theoretic pillar.  No exponential quantum speedup is known---the best quantum algorithms save a constant in the exponent, not a polynomial in the input size.  The problems have withstood over 25~years of sustained cryptanalytic attention without a breakthrough.  And they are easy to state---find short vectors in a lattice---yet exponentially hard to solve.

This combination of theoretical depth and practical maturity is why lattices, not codes or isogenies, form the backbone of the post-quantum transition.  The next chapter translates this hardness into practical cryptographic schemes via the Learning With Errors problem---a structured variant of \cvp{} that turns lattice geometry into encryption and key exchange.
