\section{The Challenge of Lattice-Based Signatures}
\label{sec:sig-challenge}

For public-key encryption, the ``encrypt in reverse'' paradigm provides a natural route from lattice problems to cryptographic schemes: the secret key holder can decode a noisy codeword that is computationally infeasible for anyone else to decode.  One might hope that a similar inversion---sign by decrypting, verify by encrypting---would yield lattice-based signatures just as easily.  It does not.

\subsection{Why ``Encrypt in Reverse'' Fails}
\label{subsec:encrypt-reverse}

In RSA, the algebraic symmetry between encryption ($m \mapsto m^e \bmod N$) and signing ($m \mapsto m^d \bmod N$) makes signatures almost free once encryption exists.  Lattice-based encryption lacks this symmetry.  The fundamental asymmetry is as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Encryption} adds a small error $\mathbf{e}$ to a lattice point; the secret key holder removes it.  The error is freshly sampled each time and does not depend on the secret key.
\item \textbf{Signing} must produce a response that depends on both the message and the secret key.  If that response involves a short vector $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{s} \cdot c + \mathbf{y}$, where $\mathbf{s}$ is the secret, $c$ the challenge, and $\mathbf{y}$ a random mask, then $\mathbf{z}$ is \emph{correlated with} $\mathbf{s}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Information Leakage Problem}
\label{subsec:info-leakage}

Consider a na\"ive scheme where the signer outputs $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + c\,\mathbf{s}$ for a uniformly random mask $\mathbf{y}$ and a challenge $c$ derived from the message.  Each signature is a sample from the distribution $\mathbf{z} \sim U + c\,\mathbf{s}$, where $U$ denotes the distribution of $\mathbf{y}$.  After $t$ signatures $(\mathbf{z}_1, c_1), \ldots, (\mathbf{z}_t, c_t)$, an adversary forms the system
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:naive-leak}
\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{z}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{z}_t \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{y}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{y}_t \end{pmatrix}
+
\begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ \vdots \\ c_t \end{pmatrix}
\mathbf{s}.
\end{equation}
Since $\mathbf{y}_i$ is bounded, the average $\frac{1}{t}\sum_i c_i^{-1}\mathbf{z}_i$ converges to $\mathbf{s}$ as $t$ grows.  More sophisticated attacks recover $\mathbf{s}$ from far fewer signatures by exploiting the distribution shift.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The core difficulty.}  A signature scheme must be \emph{deterministic} in verification (anyone can check) yet must not leak the secret across multiple uses.  For lattice schemes, this means the distribution of signatures must be \emph{independent of the secret key}---a property that does not hold for the na\"ive construction above.
\end{svgraybox}

\subsection{Two Solutions}
\label{subsec:two-solutions}

The lattice signature literature has converged on two paradigms that solve the leakage problem in fundamentally different ways:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Rejection sampling} (Sect.~\ref{sec:fs-aborts}): the signer generates a candidate signature, then \emph{rejects and restarts} with carefully controlled probability so that the output distribution is independent of $\mathbf{s}$.  This is the approach of ML-DSA / CRYSTALS-Dilithium~\cite{Dilithium2018}.

\item \textbf{Preimage sampling with trapdoors} (Sect.~\ref{sec:ntru-gpv}): the signer uses a secret ``good basis'' to sample from a discrete Gaussian centred at a message-dependent point.  Because the Gaussian distribution is spherically symmetric, the output hides the trapdoor.  This is the approach of Falcon~\cite{Falcon2020}.
\end{enumerate}

Both solutions are provably secure under standard lattice assumptions, but they lead to schemes with markedly different engineering characteristics.

\section{The Fiat-Shamir Framework}
\label{sec:fiat-shamir}

Most lattice-based signature schemes are constructed by applying the \emph{Fiat-Shamir transform}\index{Fiat-Shamir transform}~\cite{FiatShamir1986} to an interactive identification protocol.  We develop this framework in its general form before specialising to lattices.

\subsection{Interactive Identification Protocols}
\label{subsec:id-protocols}

A canonical identification scheme\index{identification scheme} is a three-move protocol between a prover $\mathcal{P}$ (who knows the secret key) and a verifier $\mathcal{V}$ (who knows only the public key):

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Commit.}  $\mathcal{P}$ samples randomness and sends a \emph{commitment} $w$ to $\mathcal{V}$.
\item \textbf{Challenge.}  $\mathcal{V}$ sends a random \emph{challenge} $c$ to $\mathcal{P}$.
\item \textbf{Response.}  $\mathcal{P}$ computes a \emph{response} $z$ using $c$, the secret key, and the commitment randomness, and sends $z$ to $\mathcal{V}$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The verifier accepts if $(w, c, z)$ satisfies a public relation.  Security requires:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Completeness:} an honest prover is always accepted.
\item \textbf{Special soundness:} given two accepting transcripts $(w, c, z)$ and $(w, c', z')$ with $c \neq c'$, one can efficiently extract the secret key.
\item \textbf{Honest-verifier zero-knowledge (HVZK):} transcripts can be simulated without the secret key.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Fiat-Shamir Transform}
\label{subsec:fs-transform}

The Fiat-Shamir transform removes interaction by replacing the verifier's random challenge with the output of a hash function applied to the commitment and the message:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fs-transform}
c \;=\; H(w \,\|\, \mu),
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the message to be signed.  The resulting non-interactive scheme is:

\begin{itemize}
\item $\KeyGen$: same as the identification scheme.
\item $\Sign(\mathsf{sk}, \mu)$: sample commitment randomness, compute $w$; set $c = H(w \| \mu)$; compute response $z$; output $\sigma = (c, z)$ (or equivalently $(w, z)$).
\item $\Verify(\mathsf{pk}, \mu, \sigma)$: recompute $w$ from $(c, z)$ using the public key; check $c = H(w \| \mu)$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Security: ROM and QROM}
\label{subsec:rom-qrom}

In the \emph{random oracle model}\index{random oracle model} (ROM), where $H$ is modelled as a truly random function, Fiat-Shamir signatures from a secure identification scheme satisfy existential unforgeability under chosen-message attacks (EU-CMA).  The proof proceeds by programming the random oracle: the simulator embeds a challenge instance into the hash responses, then uses the forking lemma to extract a forgery into two accepting transcripts with the same commitment but different challenges, breaking special soundness.

In the post-quantum setting, the adversary can query $H$ in \emph{superposition}, requiring security in the \emph{quantum random oracle model}\index{quantum random oracle model} (QROM).  The classical forking lemma fails when the adversary can query $H$ on superpositions of inputs, because measuring the query to ``rewind'' destroys the quantum state.  Several techniques address this:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Unruh's transform}~(2015): adds a commitment to the signature, increasing size but enabling a QROM proof.
\item \textbf{The measure-and-reprogram technique} of Don, Fehr, Majenz, and Schaffner~(2019): directly proves Fiat-Shamir security in the QROM for schemes with large challenge spaces.
\item \textbf{Deterministic commitments:} using a pseudorandom function (keyed by the secret key and the message) to derive the commitment randomness eliminates a subtle QROM attack vector and is the approach adopted by ML-DSA~\cite{FIPS204}.
\end{itemize}

Both ML-DSA and Falcon have proofs of EU-CMA security in the QROM, establishing their soundness against quantum adversaries.

\section{Fiat-Shamir with Aborts}
\label{sec:fs-aborts}

The na\"ive lattice identification protocol leaks the secret key through the distribution of responses (Sect.~\ref{subsec:info-leakage}).  Lyubashevsky's \emph{Fiat-Shamir with aborts}\index{Fiat-Shamir with aborts} framework~\cite{Lyubashevsky2012} solves this by adding a rejection step.

\subsection{The Protocol}
\label{subsec:fs-aborts-protocol}

Let $\mathbf{A} \in \Zq^{k \times \ell}$ be the public matrix and $\mathbf{s} \in R_q^{\ell}$ the secret key, with public key $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$.  The identification protocol proceeds as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Commit.}  Sample $\mathbf{y} \getsr S_{\gamma_1}^{\ell}$ (uniform over vectors with $\norm{\mathbf{y}}_\infty < \gamma_1$).  Compute $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$ and send the high-order bits $\mathbf{w}_1 = \mathsf{HighBits}(\mathbf{w})$.
\item \textbf{Challenge.}  Receive challenge $c \in \mathcal{C}$, where $\mathcal{C}$ is a set of polynomials with small coefficients and bounded norm.
\item \textbf{Response.}  Compute $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + c\,\mathbf{s}$.
\item \textbf{Reject.}  If $\norm{\mathbf{z}}_\infty \geq \gamma_1 - \beta$, \textbf{abort and restart from step~1}.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent The verifier checks that $\norm{\mathbf{z}}_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$ and that $\mathsf{HighBits}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - c\,\mathbf{t}) = \mathbf{w}_1$.

\subsection{Why Rejection Sampling is Necessary}
\label{subsec:rejection-necessity}

Without rejection, the response $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + c\,\mathbf{s}$ follows the distribution $U_{S_{\gamma_1}}$ shifted by $c\,\mathbf{s}$.  Different secret keys produce different shifts, and an adversary observing multiple signatures can statistically distinguish (and eventually recover) the secret.

With rejection, the signer outputs $\mathbf{z}$ only when it falls in the ``safe'' region $\norm{\mathbf{z}}_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$.  The key insight is that when $\gamma_1$ is sufficiently larger than $\norm{c\,\mathbf{s}}_\infty \leq \beta$, the conditional distribution of $\mathbf{z}$ given acceptance is \emph{nearly independent} of $\mathbf{s}$.

\subsection{The Rejection Sampling Lemma}
\label{subsec:rejection-lemma}

\begin{theorem}[Rejection Sampling Lemma~\cite{Lyubashevsky2012}]
\label{thm:rejection-sampling}
\index{rejection sampling lemma}
Let $V$ be a set of vectors with $\norm{\mathbf{v}}_\infty \leq \beta$ for all $\mathbf{v} \in V$.  Let $h: V \to [0,1]$ be a probability function and $\sigma > 0$ a parameter.  Sample $\mathbf{v} \sim h$ and $\mathbf{y} \sim D_\sigma^m$ (discrete Gaussian with parameter $\sigma$).  Define $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + \mathbf{v}$.  Then:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rejection-sampling}
\Pr\!\left[\text{output } \mathbf{z} \text{ with probability } \min\!\left(\frac{D_\sigma^m(\mathbf{z})}{M \cdot D_{\mathbf{v},\sigma}^m(\mathbf{z})},\, 1\right)\right]
\;\geq\; \frac{1}{M},
\end{equation}
where $M = \exp(12\beta/\sigma + 1/(2\sigma^2))$, and the output distribution is within statistical distance $2^{-100}/M$ of $D_\sigma^m$, \emph{independent of} $\mathbf{v}$.
\end{theorem}

The constant $M$ controls the expected number of repetitions before a signature is accepted.  In ML-DSA, parameters are chosen so that $M \approx 4$--$7$, meaning the signer restarts on average 4--7 times per signature.

\subsection{Uniform Signature Distribution}
\label{subsec:uniform-sigs}

The rejection sampling lemma guarantees that the joint distribution of $(\mathbf{z}, c)$ in accepted signatures is (statistically close to) a \emph{fixed} distribution that depends only on the public key and the message, not on the secret key.  This is the lattice analogue of the perfect zero-knowledge property in Schnorr signatures: no matter which secret key produced the signature, the output ``looks the same.''

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The efficiency-security tradeoff.}  Making $\gamma_1$ larger (relative to $\beta$) reduces the rejection probability (fewer restarts, faster signing) but increases the signature size (the vector $\mathbf{z}$ has larger entries).  Making $\gamma_1$ smaller tightens the bound, producing shorter signatures at the cost of more restarts.  ML-DSA's parameter sets (Sect.~\ref{sec:ml-dsa}) represent carefully optimised operating points on this tradeoff curve.
\end{svgraybox}

\section{ML-DSA (CRYSTALS-Dilithium)}
\label{sec:ml-dsa}

ML-DSA (Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm), standardised as FIPS~204~\cite{FIPS204}, instantiates the Fiat-Shamir-with-aborts framework over module lattices.  We present the complete scheme following the standard.

\subsection{Algebraic Setting}
\label{subsec:ml-dsa-setting}

ML-DSA works over the polynomial ring $R_q = \Z_q[X]/(X^{256}+1)$ with $q = 8\,380\,417$.  Keys and operations involve matrices and vectors of elements from $R_q$.  The module structure---vectors of ring elements rather than single ring elements---provides flexible parameterisation (Chap.~\ref{ch:lwe-encryption}).

Two hard problems underlie the security of ML-DSA:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Module-\lwe{}:} given $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_1 + \mathbf{s}_2)$, distinguish $\mathbf{t}$ from uniform, where $\mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2$ have small coefficients.
\item \textbf{Module-SIS\index{SIS!module variant}:} given $\mathbf{A}$, find a short vector $\mathbf{z}$ such that $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{0} \pmod{q}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Key Generation}
\label{subsec:ml-dsa-keygen}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ML-DSA $\KeyGen$}
\label{alg:ml-dsa-keygen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Security parameter (determining $k, \ell, \eta$)
\Ensure Public key $\mathsf{pk} = (\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{t}_1)$, secret key $\mathsf{sk} = (\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{t}_0, \mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2)$
\State Sample seed $\rho \getsr \{0,1\}^{256}$
\State Expand $\mathbf{A} \in R_q^{k \times \ell}$ from $\rho$ \Comment{Public matrix}
\State Sample $\mathbf{s}_1 \getsr S_\eta^{\ell}$, $\mathbf{s}_2 \getsr S_\eta^{k}$ \Comment{Short secret vectors}
\State $\mathbf{t} \gets \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_1 + \mathbf{s}_2$
\State $(\mathbf{t}_1, \mathbf{t}_0) \gets \mathsf{Power2Round}(\mathbf{t})$ \Comment{Split into high/low bits}
\State \Return $\mathsf{pk} = (\rho, \mathbf{t}_1)$, $\mathsf{sk} = (\rho, K, \mathsf{tr}, \mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2, \mathbf{t}_0)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The function $\mathsf{Power2Round}$ splits each coefficient of $\mathbf{t}$ into high-order bits $\mathbf{t}_1$ and low-order bits $\mathbf{t}_0$.  Only $\mathbf{t}_1$ is included in the public key, compressing it significantly; $\mathbf{t}_0$ is retained in the secret key for use during signing.

The design of key generation reflects two competing pressures.  On the one hand, the secret vectors $\mathbf{s}_1$ and $\mathbf{s}_2$ must be short enough that the Module-LWE instance is hard---if their coefficients were too large, the noise would overwhelm the structure and make decoding trivial for an adversary with lattice reduction tools.  On the other hand, they must be large enough that the rejection sampling during signing succeeds with reasonable probability; recall that the bound $\beta$ on $\norm{c \cdot \mathbf{s}}_\infty$ depends directly on the coefficient size $\eta$.  The parameter $\eta$ (either~2 or~4, depending on the security level) represents the carefully chosen balance point.  Splitting $\mathbf{t}$ into high and low parts is not merely a compression trick: it is essential to the hint mechanism used during signing, which we describe next.

\subsection{Signing}
\label{subsec:ml-dsa-sign}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ML-DSA $\Sign$}
\label{alg:ml-dsa-sign}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Message $\mu$, secret key $\mathsf{sk} = (\rho, K, \mathsf{tr}, \mathbf{s}_1, \mathbf{s}_2, \mathbf{t}_0)$
\Ensure Signature $\sigma = (\tilde{c}, \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{h})$
\State $\mathbf{A} \gets \mathsf{ExpandA}(\rho)$
\State $\mu' \gets H(\mathsf{tr} \| \mu)$ \Comment{Bind message to public key}
\State $\kappa \gets 0$
\Repeat
  \State $\mathbf{y} \gets \mathsf{ExpandMask}(\rho', \kappa)$ \Comment{Deterministic mask from $K, \mu$}
  \State $\mathbf{w} \gets \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$
  \State $\mathbf{w}_1 \gets \mathsf{HighBits}(\mathbf{w})$ \Comment{High-order bits of commitment}
  \State $\tilde{c} \gets H(\mu' \| \mathbf{w}_1)$ \Comment{Challenge hash}
  \State $c \gets \mathsf{SampleInBall}(\tilde{c})$ \Comment{Challenge polynomial, $\norm{c}_1 = \tau$}
  \State $\mathbf{z} \gets \mathbf{y} + c \cdot \mathbf{s}_1$ \Comment{Response}
  \State $\mathbf{r}_0 \gets \mathsf{LowBits}(\mathbf{w} - c \cdot \mathbf{s}_2)$
  \State $\kappa \gets \kappa + \ell$
  \If{$\norm{\mathbf{z}}_\infty \geq \gamma_1 - \beta$ \textbf{or} $\norm{\mathbf{r}_0}_\infty \geq \gamma_2 - \beta$} \Comment{Rejection check}
    \State \textbf{continue} \Comment{Abort and restart}
  \EndIf
  \State $\mathbf{h} \gets \mathsf{MakeHint}(-c\,\mathbf{t}_0,\; \mathbf{w} - c\,\mathbf{s}_2 + c\,\mathbf{t}_0)$ \Comment{Hint for verification}
  \If{$\norm{c\,\mathbf{t}_0}_\infty \geq \gamma_2$ \textbf{or} number of $1$'s in $\mathbf{h}$ $> \omega$}
    \State \textbf{continue}
  \EndIf
\Until{signature produced}
\State \Return $\sigma = (\tilde{c}, \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{h})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The algorithm repays close reading, because each step reflects a deliberate design choice---not merely a mathematical convenience.

Consider first the generation of the mask~$\mathbf{y}$.  In a classical Schnorr signature, the randomness is freshly sampled; if an implementation ever reuses a nonce, the secret key is immediately recoverable.  ML-DSA sidesteps this catastrophic failure mode by deriving~$\mathbf{y}$ deterministically from the secret seed~$K$ and the message.  Signing the same message twice always produces the same mask---and therefore the same signature---so there is no ``nonce reuse'' scenario to guard against.  This deterministic derivation also simplifies the QROM security proof: the simulator can predict the signer's randomness from the message, which is essential when the adversary can query the signing oracle on quantum superpositions of messages.

The two rejection conditions protect different secrets.  The first check, $\norm{\mathbf{z}}_\infty \geq \gamma_1 - \beta$, ensures that the response~$\mathbf{z}$ does not betray~$\mathbf{s}_1$.  Without it, large-magnitude entries in~$\mathbf{z}$ would correlate with the secret, as we saw in Sect.~\ref{subsec:info-leakage}.  The second check, $\norm{\mathbf{r}_0}_\infty \geq \gamma_2 - \beta$, is subtler: it guards~$\mathbf{s}_2$.  The low-order bits of the commitment~$\mathbf{w}$ shift when multiplied by the challenge~$c$ and the second secret vector, and if that shift is too large, the resulting signature leaks statistical information about~$\mathbf{s}_2$ through the rounding behaviour.  Both checks must pass simultaneously; failing either sends the signer back to step~1 with a fresh counter~$\kappa$.

Finally, the hint vector~$\mathbf{h}$ deserves attention because it solves a problem that arises nowhere in the abstract Fiat-Shamir framework.  The signer commits to the high-order bits~$\mathbf{w}_1$ of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$, but the verifier cannot compute $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$ (since $\mathbf{y}$ is secret).  The verifier instead computes $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - c\,\mathbf{t}$, which \emph{almost} equals $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} - c\,\mathbf{s}_2$---close enough that the high-order bits agree in most coefficients, but off by one in a handful due to carries from the low-order part.  The hint~$\mathbf{h}$ is a sparse binary vector recording exactly which coefficients need correction.  Because at most~$\omega$ coefficients differ, encoding~$\mathbf{h}$ costs far fewer bits than transmitting~$\mathbf{w}_1$ directly.  This is a small but consequential bandwidth saving that compounds across the millions of signatures a production system may generate.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{subsec:ml-dsa-verify}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ML-DSA $\Verify$}
\label{alg:ml-dsa-verify}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Public key $\mathsf{pk} = (\rho, \mathbf{t}_1)$, message $\mu$, signature $\sigma = (\tilde{c}, \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{h})$
\Ensure Accept or reject
\State $\mathbf{A} \gets \mathsf{ExpandA}(\rho)$
\State $\mu' \gets H(\mathsf{tr} \| \mu)$
\State $c \gets \mathsf{SampleInBall}(\tilde{c})$
\State $\mathbf{w}_1' \gets \mathsf{UseHint}(\mathbf{h},\; \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - c \cdot \mathbf{t}_1 \cdot 2^d)$ \Comment{Recover high bits}
\State \Return $\norm{\mathbf{z}}_\infty < \gamma_1 - \beta$ \textbf{and} $\tilde{c} = H(\mu' \| \mathbf{w}_1')$ \textbf{and} $\abs{\mathbf{h}} \leq \omega$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Verification requires only one matrix-vector multiplication $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}$, making it fast.  The check $\tilde{c} = H(\mu' \| \mathbf{w}_1')$ ensures that the recovered commitment matches the challenge.  The asymmetry between signing and verification is striking and deliberate: signing involves a rejection loop that may iterate several times, while verification is a single deterministic pass.  In most deployment scenarios---TLS handshakes, certificate chains, software updates---signatures are created once and verified many times, so front-loading the computational cost into signing is the right engineering choice.

\subsection{Hints and Compression}
\label{subsec:hints-compression}

The hint mechanism is one of ML-DSA's most elegant engineering contributions, and it illustrates a general principle in lattice cryptography: rounding is cheap but its interaction with arithmetic is treacherous.

During signing, the signer computes $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}$ and extracts its high-order bits $\mathbf{w}_1$.  The verifier, who does not know $\mathbf{y}$, instead computes $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - c\,\mathbf{t}$, which equals $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} - c\,\mathbf{s}_2$ up to the rounding decomposition.  In most coefficients, the high-order bits agree.  But wherever the low-order perturbation introduced by $c\,\mathbf{s}_2$ crosses a rounding boundary, the verifier's high bits differ from the signer's by exactly~$\pm 1$.  The hint~$\mathbf{h}$ records which coefficients suffer this carry effect, allowing the verifier to patch its computation and recover the original~$\mathbf{w}_1$.  Because $\mathbf{h}$ is sparse---at most~$\omega$ nonzero entries, typically far fewer---encoding it costs a fraction of what transmitting $\mathbf{w}_1$ directly would require.

Why not simply send $\mathbf{w}_1$ in the signature and skip the hint altogether?  Because $\mathbf{w}_1$ has $k \cdot 256$ coefficients, each requiring several bits; encoding it would add hundreds of bytes to every signature.  The hint, by contrast, needs only the positions of the nonzero entries---a list of at most $\omega$ indices---which can be encoded in roughly $\omega \cdot \lceil\log_2(k \cdot 256)\rceil$ bits.  At ML-DSA-44's parameters ($\omega = 80$), this saves over 600~bytes per signature compared to the na\"ive approach.

\subsection{Security}
\label{subsec:ml-dsa-security}

The security of ML-DSA reduces to the hardness of Module-\lwe{} and Module-SIS in the QROM.  Specifically, if there exists a quantum adversary that produces an existential forgery under a chosen-message attack, then there exists a quantum algorithm that either solves Module-\lwe{} with the scheme's parameters, or solves Module-SIS---both of which reduce (via Chap.~\ref{ch:lattice-theory}) to worst-case lattice problems.

ML-DSA's Fiat-Shamir-with-aborts approach achieves security through careful engineering of the output distribution: rejection sampling forces every signature to look as though it were drawn from a fixed distribution, regardless of which secret key produced it.  The second paradigm takes a different path entirely.  Rather than masking the secret through rejection, it exploits a geometric property of Gaussian distributions---their rotational symmetry---to hide the trapdoor in the sampling process itself.  This is mathematically cleaner but, as we shall see, far harder to implement safely.

\section{NTRU Lattices and the GPV Framework}
\label{sec:ntru-gpv}

The second paradigm for lattice signatures follows the classical hash-and-sign approach: the signer uses a trapdoor to ``invert'' a hash function.  The mathematical foundation combines NTRU lattices with the GPV (Gentry-Peikert-Vaikuntanathan) framework~\cite{GPV2008}.

\subsection{NTRU Lattices}
\label{subsec:ntru-lattices}

The NTRU cryptosystem~\cite{NTRU1998} works in the polynomial ring $R = \Z[X]/(X^n + 1)$ for $n$ a power of~$2$, with computations modulo a prime $q$.

\begin{definition}[NTRU Lattice]
\label{def:ntru-lattice}
\index{NTRU!lattice}
Given polynomials $f, g \in R$ with small coefficients and $f$ invertible modulo $q$, define the public key $h = g \cdot f^{-1} \bmod q$.  The \emph{NTRU lattice} is the $2n$-dimensional integer lattice
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ntru-lattice}
\lat_h \;=\; \bigl\{(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) \in \Z^{2n} : \mathbf{u} \equiv h \cdot \mathbf{v} \pmod{q}\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in matrix form, $\lat_h$ is generated by the rows of
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ntru-basis}
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{pub}} = \begin{pmatrix} q\,\mathbf{I}_n & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{H} & \mathbf{I}_n \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{H}$ is the anti-circulant matrix corresponding to multiplication by $h$.
\end{definition}

The pair $(f, g)$ provides a \emph{short basis} of $\lat_h$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ntru-short-basis}
\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}} = \begin{pmatrix} f & g \\ F & G \end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
where $F, G \in R$ satisfy the NTRU equation $f\,G - g\,F = q$.  The rows of $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}}$ have norm $\bigO(\sqrt{q})$, while the public basis $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{pub}}$ has a row of norm $q$---an exponential quality gap in terms of the Gram-Schmidt norms.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The NTRU hardness assumption.}  Given only $h = g\,f^{-1} \bmod q$, it is computationally infeasible to recover the short pair $(f, g)$ or any equivalently short basis of $\lat_h$.  This is closely related to the problem of finding short vectors in the NTRU lattice, and it has resisted cryptanalysis for over 25~years~\cite{NTRU1998}.
\end{svgraybox}

The exponential quality gap between the public and secret bases is what makes NTRU lattices useful for signatures.  The public basis has a row of norm~$q$---anyone can see it, but it is too ``long'' to sample short vectors efficiently.  The secret basis, with rows of norm $\bigO(\sqrt{q})$, is short enough to enable the Gaussian sampling that the GPV framework requires.  This gap is the trapdoor: a structural advantage that the key holder possesses and the adversary does not, analogous to the role of the factorisation in RSA.

\subsection{The GPV Hash-and-Sign Framework}
\label{subsec:gpv-framework}

Gentry, Peikert, and Vaikuntanathan~\cite{GPV2008} showed how to build signature schemes from any lattice equipped with a \emph{trapdoor}---a short basis that enables efficient sampling of short lattice vectors near any target point.

\begin{definition}[Hash-and-Sign Signature~\cite{GPV2008}]
\label{def:gpv-signature}
\index{GPV framework}
Let $\lat$ be a lattice with public basis $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{pub}}$ and trapdoor (short basis) $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}}$.  Let $H$ be a hash function mapping messages to points in $\R^n$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $\KeyGen$: output $\mathsf{pk} = \mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{pub}}$, $\mathsf{sk} = \mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}}$.
\item $\Sign(\mathsf{sk}, \mu)$: compute $\mathbf{c} = H(\mu)$; use $\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}}$ to sample $\mathbf{v} \sim D_{\lat, \sigma, \mathbf{c}}$ (discrete Gaussian over $\lat$ centred at $\mathbf{c}$); output $\sigma = \mathbf{v}$.
\item $\Verify(\mathsf{pk}, \mu, \sigma)$: check that $\sigma \in \lat$ and $\norm{\sigma - H(\mu)} \leq B$ for an appropriate bound $B$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Preimage Sampling}
\label{subsec:preimage-sampling}

The core algorithmic challenge is sampling from $D_{\lat, \sigma, \mathbf{c}}$---the discrete Gaussian distribution over lattice points, centred at $\mathbf{c}$, with standard deviation $\sigma$.  This distribution assigns probability proportional to $\exp(-\pi\norm{\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{c}}^2/\sigma^2)$ to each point $\mathbf{x} \in \lat$.

\begin{theorem}[GPV Preimage Sampling~\cite{GPV2008}]
\label{thm:gpv-sampling}
\index{preimage sampling}
Given a basis $\mathbf{B}$ of a lattice $\lat$ and a Gaussian parameter $\sigma \geq \norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}} \cdot \omega(\sqrt{\log n})$, where $\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}} = \max_i \norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_i}$ is the maximum Gram-Schmidt norm, there exists an efficient algorithm to sample from a distribution statistically close to $D_{\lat, \sigma, \mathbf{c}}$ for any centre $\mathbf{c}$.
\end{theorem}

The critical constraint is that $\sigma$ must exceed the Gram-Schmidt norm of the basis.  A shorter basis (smaller $\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}}$) allows a smaller $\sigma$, which in turn produces shorter signatures.  This is why the trapdoor quality---measured by $\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}}$---directly determines the scheme's efficiency.

To see why this threshold matters for security (not just correctness), consider what happens when $\sigma$ is too small.  A Gaussian with parameter $\sigma$ is concentrated within a ball of radius roughly $\sigma\sqrt{n}$.  If $\sigma$ is smaller than the longest Gram-Schmidt vector $\widetilde{\mathbf{b}}_i$, the distribution cannot ``wrap around'' the fundamental domain in the $i$-th direction: samples cluster near the centre of that coordinate, and the resulting signature distribution is no longer spherically symmetric.  An adversary collecting many signatures can detect this asymmetry and extract information about the secret basis direction by direction---precisely the kind of statistical leakage that the GPV framework is designed to prevent.  The condition $\sigma \geq \norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}} \cdot \omega(\sqrt{\log n})$ ensures that the Gaussian is wide enough to smooth over the lattice geometry in every direction, making the output statistically indistinguishable regardless of which short basis was used to produce it.

\subsection{Security of GPV Signatures}
\label{subsec:gpv-security}

\begin{theorem}[GPV Security~\cite{GPV2008}]
\label{thm:gpv-security}
If $\sigma$ is sufficiently large relative to $\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}}}}$, then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The distribution of signatures is statistically close to $D_{\lat, \sigma, H(\mu)}$, which depends only on the public lattice and the message---not on which short basis was used.  This ensures zero-knowledge.
\item Forging a signature requires finding a lattice vector close to $H(\mu)$, which is an instance of approximate \cvp{}.  The security reduces to the hardness of \sivp{} in the underlying lattice.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The GPV framework is general: it applies to any lattice with an efficiently sampleable trapdoor.  Falcon instantiates it using NTRU lattices, where the trapdoor is the short basis $(f, g, F, G)$.

\section{Falcon}
\label{sec:falcon}

Falcon (Fast-Fourier Lattice-based Compact Signatures over NTRU)~\cite{Falcon2020} combines the GPV hash-and-sign framework with NTRU lattices and a tree-based fast Fourier sampling algorithm to achieve the smallest signatures among all lattice-based schemes.

\subsection{Construction Overview}
\label{subsec:falcon-overview}

The high-level structure follows the GPV template (Definition~\ref{def:gpv-signature}), with two crucial specialisations:

\begin{enumerate}
\item The underlying lattice is an NTRU lattice $\lat_h$ (Definition~\ref{def:ntru-lattice}), whose algebraic structure enables compact keys.
\item The discrete Gaussian sampling uses a \emph{fast Fourier} decomposition of the basis, replacing the $\bigO(n^2)$ Gram-Schmidt-based sampler with an $\bigO(n \log n)$ tree-based algorithm.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Key Generation}
\label{subsec:falcon-keygen}

Key generation produces an NTRU pair with controlled quality:

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Falcon $\KeyGen$}
\label{alg:falcon-keygen}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Degree $n$ (512 or 1024)
\Ensure Public key $h$, secret key $(f, g, F, G)$ and Falcon tree $\mathcal{T}$
\Repeat
  \State Sample $f, g \getsr D_{\Z^n, \sigma_{\mathrm{kg}}}$ \Comment{Short polynomials}
  \State Check $f$ is invertible mod $q$ and mod $X^n + 1$
  \State Solve NTRU equation: find $F, G$ such that $fG - gF = q$
\Until{$\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathrm{sec}}}$ is below threshold}
\State $h \gets g \cdot f^{-1} \bmod q$
\State Precompute Falcon tree $\mathcal{T}$ from $(f, g, F, G)$ \Comment{FFT of Gram-Schmidt data}
\State \Return $\mathsf{pk} = h$, $\mathsf{sk} = \mathcal{T}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The NTRU equation $fG - gF = q$ is solved using the tower-of-fields approach: one works recursively in $\Z[X]/(X^{n/2}+1)$, then $\Z[X]/(X^{n/4}+1)$, and so on, solving a $2 \times 2$ system at each level.  This yields $F, G$ with entries of size $\bigO(q)$, which are then reduced using lattice reduction in small dimension.

Key generation is by far the most expensive phase of Falcon---it may require hundreds of milliseconds or more, compared to microseconds for ML-DSA.  This cost is acceptable because key generation is a one-time operation (or at worst infrequent), whereas signing and verification happen repeatedly over the key's lifetime.  The quality check on $\norm{\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}_{\mathrm{sec}}}$ is essential: a basis whose Gram-Schmidt norms are too large forces a wider Gaussian parameter~$\sigma$, producing longer signatures that may exceed the verification bound.  Rejecting poor-quality bases during key generation is cheaper than compensating for them in every subsequent signature.

The precomputed Falcon tree~$\mathcal{T}$ deserves emphasis.  Rather than storing the raw basis vectors and recomputing the Gram-Schmidt decomposition at each signing invocation, Falcon precomputes and stores the entire tree of FFT-domain Gram-Schmidt data.  This trades secret key storage (the tree occupies roughly~40~KB for Falcon-512) for signing speed---a worthwhile exchange in most deployment scenarios.

\subsection{Signing: The Falcon Tree}
\label{subsec:falcon-sign}

To sign a message $\mu$, the signer hashes it to a point $\mathbf{c} = H(\mu) \in \Z_q^n$ and must find a short lattice vector $\mathbf{v}$ close to $(\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{0})$ in $\lat_h$.  Equivalently, find short $(s_1, s_2) \in R^2$ such that $s_1 + s_2 h \equiv \mathbf{c} \pmod{q}$.  The signature is the short polynomial $s_2$ (from which $s_1$ can be recovered during verification).

The sampling uses the \emph{Falcon tree}\index{Falcon!tree}, a precomputed data structure that encodes the Gram-Schmidt decomposition of the NTRU basis in FFT form:

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Falcon $\Sign$ (simplified)}
\label{alg:falcon-sign}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Message $\mu$, Falcon tree $\mathcal{T}$, public key $h$
\Ensure Signature $s_2$
\State $\mathbf{c} \gets H(\mu) \bmod q$ \Comment{Hash to point}
\State $\mathbf{t} \gets (\mathbf{c}, \mathbf{0}) \cdot \mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}}^{-1}$ \Comment{Babai target in basis coords}
\State $\mathbf{z} \gets \mathsf{ffSampling}(\mathbf{t}, \mathcal{T})$ \Comment{Tree-based discrete Gaussian}
\State $\mathbf{v} \gets \mathbf{z} \cdot \mathbf{B}_{\mathrm{sec}}$ \Comment{Short lattice vector}
\State $s_2 \gets \mathbf{v}[n{:}2n]$ \Comment{Second component}
\If{$\norm{(s_1, s_2)} > B$}
  \State \textbf{restart} \Comment{Rare: norm exceeded bound}
\EndIf
\State Compress and output $s_2$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The key subroutine $\mathsf{ffSampling}$ exploits the recursive structure of the ring $R = \Z[X]/(X^n+1)$.  Since $X^n + 1 = (X^{n/2} + \zeta)(X^{n/2} - \zeta)$ over a suitable extension, the Gram-Schmidt decomposition splits into two half-sized subproblems.  Applied recursively, this yields a binary tree of depth $\log_2 n$, with a one-dimensional Gaussian sample at each leaf.  The total cost is $\bigO(n \log n)$ operations---the same complexity as an FFT.

The contrast with ML-DSA's signing procedure is instructive.  ML-DSA uses rejection sampling: generate a candidate, check whether it leaks information, and restart if it does.  Falcon avoids rejection almost entirely (the norm check on line~6 triggers with probability less than $10^{-4}$ under typical parameters).  Instead, the Gaussian sampling procedure \emph{inherently} produces output that is independent of the secret basis, provided $\sigma$ is large enough.  This makes Falcon's signing faster in terms of expected iterations---but each iteration is more expensive, requiring $\bigO(n \log n)$ floating-point operations rather than the modular integer arithmetic of ML-DSA.

\subsection{Verification}
\label{subsec:falcon-verify}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Falcon $\Verify$}
\label{alg:falcon-verify}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Public key $h$, message $\mu$, signature $s_2$
\Ensure Accept or reject
\State $\mathbf{c} \gets H(\mu) \bmod q$
\State $s_1 \gets \mathbf{c} - s_2 \cdot h \bmod q$
\State \Return $\norm{(s_1, s_2)}^2 \leq B^2$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Verification is extremely efficient: one polynomial multiplication, one subtraction, and a norm check.  No tree structure or Gaussian sampling is needed.

\subsection{The Constant-Time Challenge}
\label{subsec:falcon-constant-time}

Falcon's primary implementation difficulty is that the $\mathsf{ffSampling}$ algorithm requires high-precision floating-point arithmetic---specifically, IEEE~754 double-precision (53-bit significand) at minimum.  This requirement creates a cascade of engineering problems that, taken together, make Falcon one of the hardest cryptographic primitives to implement safely.

The deepest problem is timing.  On most processors, floating-point operations execute in constant time for ``normal'' operands but slow down dramatically when an operand is subnormal---a value so small that its leading significand bit is zero.  The CPU either handles the subnormal in microcode (adding dozens of cycles) or raises a trap that the operating system resolves in software (adding thousands).  During $\mathsf{ffSampling}$, the Gram-Schmidt coefficients at deeper levels of the Falcon tree can become extremely small, especially for certain secret bases, and the intermediate products of these coefficients can land in the subnormal range.  An attacker measuring signature generation time can detect these slowdowns and, from their pattern, reconstruct information about the secret tree---and therefore the secret basis.  Multiple academic works have demonstrated precisely this attack against Falcon implementations.

The problem compounds across platforms.  The IEEE~754 standard permits implementations to differ in their treatment of edge cases: flush-to-zero modes, rounding tie-breaking, and the handling of infinities and NaNs are all implementation-defined in certain contexts.  A Falcon implementation that produces correct signatures on one CPU may produce subtly incorrect ones on another, or---worse---may produce signatures that are correct but whose timing profile reveals the secret key on one platform but not on another.  Testing for constant-time behaviour requires not just code review but platform-specific timing analysis, often at the microarchitectural level.

Constrained devices present a separate barrier.  Many embedded processors---ARM Cortex-M0/M3 microcontrollers, for example, which are the workhorses of IoT and smart-card applications---lack a hardware floating-point unit entirely.  On these platforms, every floating-point operation must be emulated in software, multiplying the signing cost by one to two orders of magnitude and often exceeding the real-time budgets that embedded applications demand.  ML-DSA, which uses only modular integer arithmetic, faces no such limitation.

Finally, the tree traversal itself poses a side-channel risk beyond arithmetic timing.  The $\mathsf{ffSampling}$ procedure accesses different nodes of the precomputed Falcon tree depending on the intermediate sampling results, which in turn depend on the secret basis.  On processors with shared caches, an attacker co-located on the same hardware can observe cache-line access patterns and infer which tree nodes were visited---a classic cache-timing attack.  Mitigating this requires loading the entire tree into cache before each signing operation or using constant-time memory access primitives, both of which add complexity and cost.

NIST's standardisation of Falcon (as FN-DSA) includes explicit guidance that implementers must ensure constant-time behaviour of all floating-point operations---a requirement that few development teams can confidently meet without specialised expertise and extensive side-channel testing.

\section{ML-DSA vs Falcon: A Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:sig-comparison}

The two NIST lattice-based signature standards embody fundamentally different answers to the same question: how should a signer prove knowledge of a short lattice vector without revealing it?  ML-DSA says: compute a response, check whether it leaks information, and retry if it does.  Falcon says: sample from a distribution that is inherently independent of the secret, using a trapdoor that only the key holder possesses.  These different philosophies propagate into every aspect of the resulting schemes---size, speed, implementation difficulty, and deployment suitability.

\subsection{Size vs.\ Simplicity}
\label{subsec:size-vs-simplicity}

The most immediately visible difference is size.  Falcon-512 signatures are roughly $3.6\times$ smaller than ML-DSA-44 signatures, and the combined public-key-plus-signature footprint is less than half.  In protocols where signatures are transmitted frequently---TLS handshakes, certificate chains, blockchain transactions---this difference compounds.  A certificate chain with three signatures saves roughly 5~KB per connection by switching from ML-DSA to Falcon, which can matter at scale or over constrained links.

But size is not free.  Falcon achieves its compact signatures through the GPV framework's Gaussian sampling, which requires the floating-point arithmetic and tree-based data structures discussed in Sect.~\ref{subsec:falcon-constant-time}.  ML-DSA, by contrast, uses only modular integer arithmetic---addition, multiplication, and comparison in $\Z_q$---which is straightforward to implement in constant time on any platform.  The engineering cost of getting Falcon right is not just higher; it is qualitatively different, requiring expertise in floating-point microarchitecture that most cryptographic engineering teams do not possess.

\subsection{Performance Characteristics}
\label{subsec:perf-characteristics}

Raw throughput numbers can mislead.  Falcon signs faster than ML-DSA (roughly $5\,000$ vs.\ $3\,700$ operations per second on a modern desktop) and verifies roughly $3\times$ faster.  But ML-DSA's signing speed is highly predictable---each attempt takes the same time, and the rejection loop adds only a constant expected factor.  Falcon's signing time, while fast on average, depends on the floating-point behaviour of the specific secret key and message, creating a variable timing profile that an attacker can exploit unless constant-time countermeasures are in place.

For verification, Falcon's advantage is clear and comes with no side-channel risk: the verifier performs one polynomial multiplication and a norm check, with no secret data involved.  In applications where verification dominates---public ledgers, broadcast authentication---this advantage is significant.

\subsection{Deployment Considerations}
\label{subsec:deployment-considerations}

For the vast majority of applications, ML-DSA is the right choice.  Its implementation simplicity, natural constant-time behaviour, and broad platform support---including embedded processors without floating-point hardware---make it the safer engineering decision.  NIST designates ML-DSA as the primary standard for digital signatures, and this designation reflects not a judgement about mathematical elegance but a pragmatic assessment of where implementations are most likely to be correct.

Falcon earns its place in a narrower but important niche: applications where signature size dominates all other concerns.  Blockchain ledgers that store millions of signatures, certificate transparency logs, bandwidth-constrained satellite links---in these settings, the $3.6\times$ size reduction over ML-DSA may justify the additional engineering investment.  Any deployment of Falcon should be backed by expert cryptographic engineering and rigorous side-channel testing; this is not a scheme where a competent but non-specialist team can safely ``roll their own'' implementation.

\begin{svgraybox}
\textbf{The diversity argument.}  Beyond performance and size, standardising two schemes based on different lattice problems---Module-LWE/SIS for ML-DSA, NTRU for Falcon---provides a cryptographic hedge.  If a breakthrough algorithm were discovered against one problem family, the other scheme could serve as a fallback.  Cryptographic diversity is not redundancy; it is insurance.  This consideration was a key factor in NIST's decision to standardise both.
\end{svgraybox}

\subsection{The Broader Landscape}
\label{subsec:broader-landscape}

The lattice-based schemes do not exhaust the post-quantum signature design space.  NIST has also standardised SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+), a hash-based signature scheme that requires no lattice assumptions at all (Chap.~\ref{ch:hash-signatures}).  SLH-DSA's security rests solely on the collision resistance of its underlying hash function---an assumption that is both older and more conservative than any lattice problem.  The price is size: SLH-DSA signatures range from roughly 7 to 49~KB, an order of magnitude larger than either lattice-based scheme.

Together, the three standards span the design space along a clear axis.  ML-DSA occupies the practical centre: moderate sizes, straightforward implementation, broad applicability.  Falcon trades implementation complexity for compact signatures.  SLH-DSA trades signature size for the strongest possible security assumptions.  The choice among them is not a matter of which is ``best'' in the abstract, but which tradeoff profile matches the constraints of the application at hand.
